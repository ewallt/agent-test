The Drinker Paradox

A theorem in first-order logic that confounds natural language

The Drinker Paradox

The Drinker Paradox is a theorem in first-order logic, popularised by the logician Raymond Smullyan. It sounds absurd at first, but follows rigorously from the semantics of material implication.

The Theorem

In any non-empty bar, there exists a person such that: if that person is drinking, then everyone in the bar is drinking.

∃x [ D(x) → ∀y D(y) ]

where D(x) means "x is drinking" and the domain is the set of people in the bar.

Why It Seems Wrong

The statement appears to claim a causal link — that one special person's drinking causes everyone else to drink. That is not what it claims. It is a purely logical assertion about a conditional's truth value, not about causation.

The Proof

  1. Case 1 — Not everyone is drinking. Pick any non-drinker and call them x. Since x is not drinking, the antecedent D(x) is false. A conditional with a false antecedent is vacuously true, regardless of the consequent. Therefore ∃x [ D(x) → ∀y D(y) ] holds.
  2. Case 2 — Everyone is drinking. Pick anyone and call them x. Both D(x) and ∀y D(y) are true, so the conditional is true. Therefore ∃x [ D(x) → ∀y D(y) ] holds.
  3. Conclusion. Those two cases are jointly exhaustive and mutually exclusive. The theorem holds in every non-empty bar.
Key Insight

The proof exploits vacuous truth: a material conditional P → Q is true whenever P is false, regardless of what Q says. The "special person" is either a non-drinker (Case 1) or any person when everyone drinks (Case 2).

Historical Note

Raymond Smullyan introduced this theorem in his 1978 book What Is the Name of This Book? as an illustration of how logic can produce results that feel paradoxical but are rigorously provable. It belongs to a family of results in classical logic that hinge on the non-intuitive behaviour of material implication.

The If/Then Problem

The Drinker Paradox is easy to state in symbolic logic. The challenge is stating it in ordinary English in a way that causes a listener to give the logically correct answer. This turns out to be surprisingly hard.

The Natural Attempt

Candidate Question

"If you are drinking, is everyone drinking?"

Asked of a non-drinker, this should elicit yes — their antecedent is false, so the conditional is vacuously true. Instead, they say no, or "that's a strange question." Why?

Gricean Implicature

The philosopher Paul Grice identified a set of conversational maxims that speakers follow by default. Central among them is the Maxim of Relevance: say only what is pertinent. When a listener hears "if P then Q," they assume the speaker believes P is possible and that P is relevant to Q.

What logic saysWhat the listener hears
P → Q is true when P is false"The speaker thinks I might be drinking"
No causal claim is made"The speaker implies my drinking causes universal drinking"
Vacuous truth is a legitimate case"If P doesn't apply to me, the question is unanswerable"

The Causal / Temporal Reading

English "if…then" is overwhelmingly used to express causal or temporal sequences. When a listener hears "If you are drinking, is everyone drinking?" they parse it as a counterfactual about a change of state: "If I were to start drinking, would that cause everyone to drink?" — not an assertion about the present moment.

The Root Problem

The non-drinker hears "if P happens, then Q happens" — a causal claim — rather than "if P is currently true, then Q is currently true" — a statement about present conditions.

Approaches That Fail

Hypothetical framing

"Supposing you were drinking, would everyone be?"

✗ Explicitly counterfactual. Invites the wrong reasoning.

Modal framing

"Could it be that you're drinking without everyone drinking?"

✗ Asks about possibility, not the conditional itself.

Guarantee framing

"Can you guarantee that if you drink, everyone drinks?"

✗ Still sounds like a claim about future action.

Only-if framing

"Are you drinking only if everyone is drinking?"

✗ Logically correct, but most people answer "no, I drink independently."

The Negation Strategy That Works

The negation of P → Q is P ∧ ¬Q. Asking someone to rule out that formulation is logically equivalent and lands more naturally:

Working version — ¬(P ∧ ¬Q) form

"Can you rule out that you are drinking and someone else is not?"

A non-drinker says yes, I can rule that out — correctly, because the first conjunct is already false. A drinker in a full room also says yes. This works because people are comfortable denying a conjunction. However it uses no if/then language.

The If/Then Version — With an Explicit Escape Hatch

To preserve the if/then language while still eliciting the correct answer, we can surface the vacuously-true case explicitly as an acceptable response:

Working if/then version

"If you are drinking, is everyone drinking? — or does that not apply to you? Say yes if it doesn't apply, or if everyone is drinking."

This is logically honest: the two conditions under which a person should say yes are precisely the conditions under which P → Q is true. The explicit escape hatch makes the vacuous case visible and acceptable to ordinary speakers.

The Bar Tab Solution

There is a more elegant reformulation that requires no escape hatch, no logical notation, and no explanation — and yet is logically equivalent to the Drinker Paradox.

The Setup

House Rule
If everyone in the bar is drinking, drinks are on the house.
Claim
There is someone in this bar who will not have to pay.

Why the Claim Is Always True

  1. Not everyone is drinking. At least one person is not drinking. That person has nothing to pay for — they will not have to pay. The claim holds.
  2. Everyone is drinking. The house rule triggers. Drinks are on the house. Nobody pays. The claim holds.
  3. Conclusion. Those two cases are exhaustive. The claim is always true.
Key Move

The non-drinker's "not applicable" case is handled silently and naturally: they simply have nothing to pay for. They never need to reason about a false antecedent. The vacuous truth disappears into ordinary thinking about who owes what.

Truth Tables

A truth table is a systematic way of showing the truth value of a compound logical statement for every possible combination of truth values of its components. The components are propositional variables — placeholders that can be either T (true) or F (false).

In the context of this paradox, we use two variables:

P = "You are drinking" Q = "Everyone is drinking"

Each row of a truth table represents one possible state of the world. With two variables there are exactly four combinations.

Negation: ¬P

The simplest operation. Negation flips the truth value. If P is true, ¬P is false, and vice versa.

Conjunction: P ∧ Q

Read as "P and Q." The conjunction is true only when both P and Q are true. In everyday terms: "You are drinking and everyone is drinking."

In plain English

P ∧ Q is true in exactly one case: when both statements are simultaneously true. If either is false, the "and" fails.

Disjunction: P ∨ Q

Read as "P or Q." The disjunction is true when at least one of P or Q is true. This is inclusive or — it includes the case where both are true. In everyday terms: "Either you are drinking or everyone is drinking (or both)."

PQP ∨ QNotes

TFTP is true → disjunction holds
FTTQ is true → disjunction holds
FFFBoth false → only case that fails
In plain English

P ∨ Q is false in exactly one case: when both statements are simultaneously false. It is the "mirror image" of conjunction — true almost always, failing only when everything fails at once.

De Morgan's Laws

Two fundamental equivalences connect conjunction, disjunction, and negation. They allow us to "push" a negation inside a compound statement by flipping the connective:

¬(P ∧ Q) ≡ ¬P ∨ ¬Q ¬(P ∨ Q) ≡ ¬P ∧ ¬Q

In words: "not (both)" is the same as "at least one is not." These laws are essential for proving the equivalences in the next tab.

Three Equivalent Forms

The core claim of the Drinker Paradox — "if you are drinking, then everyone is drinking" — can be expressed in three logically equivalent ways. Each has the same truth table; they differ only in how naturally they translate into English questions.

Throughout: P = "You are drinking", Q = "Everyone is drinking."

The standard conditional. True in all cases except when P is true and Q is false — that is, when you are drinking but not everyone is. The troublesome rows 3 and 4 are vacuously true: the antecedent is false, so the conditional cannot be violated.

Every row matches across all three columns. The three forms are logically equivalent — any statement that is true in one form is true in all three.

The Chain of Equivalences

P → Q ≡ ¬P ∨ Q (definition of material implication) ≡ ¬P ∨ ¬¬Q (double negation) ≡ ¬(P ∧ ¬Q) (De Morgan's law)

Why This Matters for the Paradox

Each form translates into a different natural-language question for the bar. Since they are logically equivalent, they all express the same claim — but they differ significantly in how comprehensible and natural they feel to ordinary speakers. Bar Scenario: ¬P ∨ Q

We ask each person in the bar a yes/no question whose correct answer is yes exactly when ¬P ∨ Q holds for that person. The paradox guarantees at least one person must answer yes.

The Question
"Is it the case that either you are not drinking, or everyone is drinking?"
If No: "No — I am drinking, and not everyone is."

Set up the room, then step through the questioning. Watch how each "no" answer confirms that person is drinking — and why the last person cannot also say "no."

Bar Scenario: ¬(P ∧ ¬Q)

The same logical claim, restated as a negated conjunction. The "no" answer is deliberately rephrased as a positive affirmation to avoid a confusing double negative.

The Question
"Is it not the case that you are drinking but someone else is not?"
If No: "It IS the case that I am drinking but someone else is not."

Logically identical to Tab VII — the same people say yes and no — but the phrasing makes the structure of the claim more explicit. Notice how the "no" response exposes the inner conjunction P ∧ ¬Q directly.
