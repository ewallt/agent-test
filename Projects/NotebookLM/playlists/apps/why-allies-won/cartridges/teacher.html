Additional facts by tab: Hero tab: The book that inspired this app, Richard Overy's "Why the Allies Won" (1995), was written partly as a direct rebuttal to the "inevitability" thesis — the widespread assumption among historians that the Allies' overwhelming material advantage made the outcome a foregone conclusion. Overy argued this was a retrospective illusion: in 1942 the outcome was genuinely uncertain, and the factors that decided it were choices, not just numbers. Thesis tab: The economic disparity between the Allies and Axis in 1942 was real but widely misunderstood. Germany did not lack resources — it had looted the industrial capacity of most of continental Europe. What it lacked was the organizational ability to mobilize those resources efficiently. Albert Speer, appointed Armaments Minister in 1942, actually doubled German war production in two years — but by then the Allies had already built a production lead that was structurally impossible to close. Intelligence War tab: The intelligence advantage at Bletchley Park almost failed before it began. In February 1942, the German navy introduced a fourth rotor to Naval Enigma, blacking out Allied codebreakers for nearly ten months. During that blackout, U-boat sinkings spiked catastrophically — over 500,000 tons of Allied shipping lost in a single month at the peak. The eventual break back into Naval Enigma in December 1942 was enabled partly by physical capture of cipher materials from German vessels, not just mathematical analysis. Air War tab: The P-51 Mustang's transformation into a long-range escort fighter almost didn't happen. The original Mustang used an Allison engine that degraded badly at high altitude, making it useless for deep escort missions. It was a Royal Air Force engineer who suggested fitting it with the Rolls-Royce Merlin engine — a modification that nearly tripled its effective range and turned a mediocre aircraft into arguably the most decisive single weapon of the air war over Europe. Production tab: The Soviet industrial evacuation of 1941 and 1942 was one of the largest logistical operations in history. Over 1,500 factories were dismantled, loaded onto railcars, and moved east of the Ural Mountains — some in as little as two weeks — as German forces advanced. Workers often resumed production before the factory walls had been rebuilt, working in open fields in winter conditions. This relocation preserved the industrial base that made the T-34 production numbers possible. Conclusion tab: The Allied victory created a historical irony that Overy emphasizes: the organizational and technological lessons that won the war — mass standardization, systems thinking, integrated logistics — became the foundation of postwar American and Soviet industrial dominance. Germany, which had the most sophisticated engineering culture in the world, lost partly because it was too attached to engineering excellence. The Allies won by valuing throughput over perfection.
