This is a self-defeating logic: preserving operational strength in the short term by cannibalizing the long-term replacement pool. Standard: The production paradox is that the USSR outproduced Germany in tanks and aircraft in 1942, the year it lost its industrial heartland. The answer was standardization: the Soviets built a few proven designs (T-34, Il-2) at massive volume using unskilled labor, while Germany produced over 425 aircraft variants and 151 truck variants — complexity that made scaling and maintenance impossible. Deep: The German production failure is a case study in the cost of perfectionism in a system under pressure. German engineers and generals consistently prioritized capability improvements over production stability — new variants, upgraded specifications, engineering refinements that reset tooling and training each time. Albert Speer's armaments rationalization after 1942 made significant gains, but it was fighting against deeply embedded institutional culture. The Soviet model was the opposite: accept a design that is good enough, freeze it, and build it faster than the enemy can destroy it. The T-34 was not the best tank in 1942 — it had significant ergonomic and mechanical problems — but it was buildable at scale, and scale was what mattered. This connects back to the determinism argument: raw industrial capacity only becomes an advantage if you organize it correctly, which is precisely what the Soviets did and the Germans did not.
