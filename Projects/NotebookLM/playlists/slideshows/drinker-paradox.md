### Slide Structure (N=10)

**1. Slide 1: A Strange Guarantee**
*   **Title:** In Any Pub, Someone Holds the Key
*   **Paragraph A:** Walk into any pub — any pub at all, anywhere in the world — and something remarkable is logically guaranteed. There exists at least one person in that pub such that if that person is drinking, then everyone in the pub is drinking. Not probably. Not usually. Necessarily, as a matter of pure logic.
*   **Paragraph B:** This is the Drinker Paradox, a theorem of classical first-order logic. It sounds like a magic trick or a piece of barroom nonsense. It is neither. It is a rigorously provable statement — and understanding why it is true teaches us something deep about the difference between logic and common sense.

**2. Slide 2: The Statement in Formal Logic**
*   **Title:** ∃x ( D(x) → ∀y D(y) )
*   **Paragraph A:** In the language of first-order logic, the Drinker Paradox reads: there exists a person x such that if x is drinking, then for all persons y, y is drinking. D(x) means "x is drinking." The arrow means "if...then." The quantifiers ∃ and ∀ mean "there exists" and "for all."
*   **Paragraph B:** This is not a statement about any particular person. It does not say who the person is. It says only that such a person must exist — that the pub cannot be arranged in any way that falsifies this. Whatever combination of drinkers and non-drinkers you place in the pub, the theorem holds.

**3. Slide 3: The Proof Begins — Two Cases**
*   **Title:** Either Everyone Is Drinking, or Someone Is Not
*   **Paragraph A:** The proof uses one of the oldest tools in classical logic: the Law of Excluded Middle. For any proposition, either it is true or its negation is true — no third option exists. Applied here: either everyone in the pub is currently drinking, or at least one person is not.
*   **Paragraph B:** These two cases are exhaustive and mutually exclusive. The proof shows that in either case, we can find the witness the theorem requires. We do not need to know which case we are in before we begin — the proof handles both, and together they cover every possible state of the pub.

**4. Slide 4: Case One — Everyone Is Drinking**
*   **Title:** When the Whole Pub Is Drinking, Any Person Will Do
*   **Paragraph A:** Suppose everyone in the pub is drinking. Then "for all y, D(y)" is already true — the consequent of our conditional is satisfied regardless of anything else. In this situation, pick any person at all. Call them P. The statement "if P is drinking, then everyone is drinking" is true, because everyone is drinking anyway.
*   **Paragraph B:** The conclusion is trivially satisfied. Any patron can serve as our witness. We have found a person — in fact, every person — for whom the conditional holds. Case one is closed: the theorem is confirmed.

**5. Slide 5: Case Two — Someone Is Not Drinking**
*   **Title:** The Non-Drinker Is Our Witness
*   **Paragraph A:** Now suppose at least one person is not drinking. Call this person Q. Q's glass is empty, or Q is nursing a water. D(Q) is false. Now consider the conditional: "if Q is drinking, then everyone is drinking." The antecedent — Q is drinking — is false.
*   **Paragraph B:** In classical logic, a conditional with a false antecedent is always true. It is called vacuously true. The only way this conditional could be false is if Q were drinking while someone else was not — but Q is not drinking, so that situation cannot arise. Q is our witness. The theorem is confirmed again.

**6. Slide 6: The Material Conditional**
*   **Title:** "If P Then Q" Is Not What You Think It Is
*   **Paragraph A:** The key to the paradox is the material conditional — the logical operator written P → Q. In formal logic, P → Q is false in exactly one case: when P is true and Q is false. In all other combinations, including when P is false, P → Q is true. This is the complete definition. No causation is implied, no relationship, no relevance.
*   **Paragraph B:** This differs fundamentally from how we use "if...then" in everyday speech. When we say "if it rains, I will bring an umbrella," we imply intention and cause. Formal logic strips all of that away. The material conditional asks only one question: is P true while Q is false? If not, the conditional holds.

**7. Slide 7: Vacuous Truth**
*   **Title:** True Because It Cannot Be Falsified
*   **Paragraph A:** When the antecedent of a conditional is false, the conditional is called vacuously true. "If the moon is made of cheese, then 2 + 2 = 5" is vacuously true. "If this person is drinking, then everyone is drinking" — said of a person who is not drinking — is vacuously true. Neither statement tells us anything meaningful. But both are true.
*   **Paragraph B:** Vacuous truth is not a flaw in formal logic. It is a deliberate and necessary consequence of how the material conditional is defined. Removing it would create far worse problems. The Drinker Paradox exploits vacuous truth directly — the non-drinker witness works precisely because their conditional cannot be falsified.

**8. Slide 8: Non-Constructive Existence**
*   **Title:** We Know Someone Exists, But Cannot Name Them in Advance**
*   **Paragraph A:** The Drinker proof is non-constructive. It does not identify the witness before surveying the pub. In Case 1, any person works — but we needed to confirm everyone was drinking first. In Case 2, any non-drinker works — but we needed to find one first. The identity of the witness depends on the state of the pub.
*   **Paragraph B:** Constructive mathematicians, who follow intuitionistic logic, reject this kind of proof. They require that an existence claim actually exhibit a specific witness. Intuitionistic logic does not accept the Law of Excluded Middle as an axiom, so the two-case structure of this proof does not go through. The Drinker Paradox is valid in classical logic and invalid in intuitionistic logic — a rare and illuminating division.

**9. Slide 9: Raymond Smullyan**
*   **Title:** The Logician Who Made Paradoxes Playful
*   **Paragraph A:** The Drinker Paradox is widely attributed to Raymond Smullyan (1919–2017), an American logician, mathematician, magician, and Taoist philosopher. His books — including "What Is the Name of This Book?" (1978) and "To Mock a Mockingbird" (1985) — introduced millions of readers to formal logic through puzzles and paradoxes. He studied under Emil Post and held positions at Princeton and CUNY.
*   **Paragraph B:** Smullyan believed that rigor and playfulness reinforce each other rather than conflict. The Drinker Paradox is a perfect expression of that belief: a statement that sounds absurd at a pub quiz, proves out cleanly under logical examination, and in doing so teaches something genuine and lasting about how formal reasoning works.

**10. Slide 10: What the Paradox Teaches**
*   **Title:** Formal Logic and Everyday Intuition Are Different Games
*   **Paragraph A:** The Drinker Paradox is not really about pubs or drinking. It is about the gap between the material conditional and everyday "if...then" reasoning, between classical and intuitionistic logic, and between constructive and non-constructive existence. It shows that formal logic can guarantee the existence of something without telling us who or what it is.
*   **Paragraph B:** The feeling of paradox dissolves the moment you understand the material conditional and vacuous truth. What seemed like magic — one person whose drinking status controls everyone else's — turns out to be a precise, clean theorem with a two-line proof. That journey from confusion to clarity is what recreational logic, at its best, always delivers.
