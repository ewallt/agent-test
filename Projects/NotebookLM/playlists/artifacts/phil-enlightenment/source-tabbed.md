# Knowledge Base: Philosophical Revolutions — Enlightenment
## Source for Tabbed Web App

---

TAB: Doubt Everything
QUOTE: "I have convinced myself that there is absolutely nothing in the world, no sky, no earth, no minds, no bodies. Doesn't it follow that I don't exist? No, surely I must exist if it's me who is convinced of something."
— René Descartes, Meditations on First Philosophy
EXPLANATION: Descartes set out to find a foundation for knowledge that was beyond all possible doubt, systematically dismantling every belief that could possibly be questioned — his senses, the physical world, even mathematics — imagining an all-powerful evil demon deceiving him at every turn. What survived this assault became the bedrock of his philosophy. The very act of doubting proved the existence of a doubter: "I think, therefore I am." From this single undeniable truth, Descartes rebuilt philosophy from scratch.

TAB: Reason Has Limits
QUOTE: "Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions, and can never pretend to any other office than to serve and obey them."
— David Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature
EXPLANATION: Hume attacked the rationalist tradition at its root, arguing that all knowledge derives from sensory experience and that reason alone can establish nothing about the world. His most provocative claim was that reason is powerless to motivate action or ground morality — it can only serve the desires and passions we already have. Hume also demolished the idea of causation as a logical necessity: when we observe one event following another, we never perceive any "necessary connection" — only habit and expectation, repeated enough times to feel inevitable.

TAB: The Self Is an Illusion
QUOTE: "For my part, when I enter most intimately into what I call myself, I always stumble on some particular perception or other... I never can catch myself at any time without a perception, and never can observe any thing but the perception."
— David Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature
EXPLANATION: Hume turned his empiricism inward and found no enduring self — only a stream of fleeting perceptions. The unified soul that Descartes had placed at the center of his philosophy simply did not appear when Hume looked for it. The "self" is nothing more than a bundle of perceptions in constant flux — a convenient fiction, not a discoverable fact. This bundle theory of the self remains one of the most radical and unsettling ideas in all of philosophy.

TAB: The Mind Shapes Reality
QUOTE: "Hitherto it has been assumed that all our knowledge must conform to objects. But... we must therefore make trial whether we may not have more success in the tasks of metaphysics, if we suppose that objects must conform to our knowledge."
— Immanuel Kant, Critique of Pure Reason
EXPLANATION: Kant's response to Hume's skepticism was to invert the relationship between mind and world. Rather than assuming the mind passively receives reality, Kant argued it actively structures experience — imposing its own forms of space, time, and causality onto the raw data of the senses. Just as Copernicus resolved astronomy by moving the Earth rather than the sun, Kant resolved epistemology by making objects conform to the mind. This is why mathematics and physics are necessarily true: they describe the structures the mind itself provides.

TAB: Dare to Know
QUOTE: "Enlightenment is man's emergence from his self-imposed immaturity. Immaturity is the inability to use one's understanding without guidance from another. Sapere Aude! Have courage to use your own understanding! — that is the motto of enlightenment."
— Immanuel Kant, "What Is Enlightenment?" (1784)
EXPLANATION: Kant's most accessible essay defined the Enlightenment as a demand for intellectual courage: the willingness to think for oneself rather than defer to authority, tradition, or the guidance of others. Most people remain intellectually immature, he argued, not from lack of ability but from lack of resolve — it is easier and more comfortable to let others do the thinking. Kant's call to Sapere Aude — dare to know — remains the clearest statement of what the Enlightenment project was ultimately about.
