TAB: Newton's Absolute World
QUOTE: "Absolute time, without reference to anything external, flows uniformly."
— Isaac Newton, Principia Mathematica
EXPLANATION: Newton's absolute space and time are often dismissed as metaphysical blunders — but they were carefully reasoned definitions required to make physics possible. Descartes' theory of relative motion was ambiguous: if all motion is relative to other bodies, there is no way to define the inertial frames that Newton's laws require. Newton's bucket experiment showed that rotation has real, observable effects — the shape of the water surface — that cannot be explained by relative motion alone. Absolute space and time were not guesses; they were the foundation that made the Principia work.

TAB: Entropy Is Not Disorder
QUOTE: "It is as though the ground had been pulled out from under one, with no firm foundation to be seen anywhere, upon which one could have built."
— Albert Einstein, on the state of physics before relativity
EXPLANATION: The popular idea that entropy equals disorder is simply wrong — and has caused decades of confusion. Classical thermodynamics defines entropy through heat flow over reversible paths; statistical mechanics defines it through the number of microscopic states corresponding to a given macroscopic condition. Neither definition equates entropy to spatial messiness. In many processes, increasing entropy produces highly ordered structures: crystals, snowflakes, biological complexity. The real content of the Second Law is about the direction of energy flow and the limits of converting heat to work — not about tidiness.

TAB: Einstein Redefines Time
QUOTE: "The concept of simultaneity does not exist for the physicist until he has the possibility of discovering whether it is fulfilled in an actual case."
— Albert Einstein, On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies (1905)
EXPLANATION: Einstein's Special Relativity began with a simple question: what does it mean for two events to happen "at the same time"? His answer was operational — simultaneity must be defined by a physical procedure, not assumed as an absolute given. Using light signals as the synchronizing standard, he showed that two events simultaneous in one inertial frame are not simultaneous in a moving frame. This was not a measurement error: it was a fundamental feature of spacetime, with consequences including time dilation, length contraction, and E = mc².

TAB: Gravity as Geometry
QUOTE: "[We] are bound to admit that it is in four dimensions that the relations being considered here first reveal their inner essence in full simplicity."
— Hermann Minkowski, on spacetime geometry
EXPLANATION: General Relativity replaced Newton's gravitational force with a curved four-dimensional spacetime. The equivalence principle — that gravitational and inertial acceleration are locally indistinguishable — led Einstein to the conclusion that mass curves spacetime, and curved spacetime tells matter how to move. Confirmed by Eddington's observation of light bending around the sun in 1919, GR has passed every experimental test since. Yet remarkably, multiple distinct theoretical frameworks — including teleparallel theories using torsion instead of curvature — reproduce every one of GR's predictions, raising deep questions about what we are actually discovering.

TAB: Science Doesn't Just Accumulate
QUOTE: "A new scientific truth does not triumph by convincing its opponents and making them see the light, but rather because its opponents eventually die, and a new generation grows up that is familiar with it."
— Max Planck
EXPLANATION: Thomas Kuhn showed that science advances not by smooth accumulation but through periodic revolutions — paradigm shifts that replace one entire framework with an incompatible successor. Normal science is puzzle-solving within an accepted paradigm; when anomalies accumulate, crisis follows, and eventually a revolution. Steven Weinberg pushed back: while interpretive frameworks change, the mathematical "hard parts" of mature theories (Newton's equations, Maxwell's equations) survive as permanent discoveries, incorporated and bounded by what comes next. The debate between Kuhn and Weinberg is ultimately about whether physics converges on truth or merely on usefulness.
