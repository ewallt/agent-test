Tuvalu — The Nation Preparing to Disappear

Tuvalu is a Pacific island nation of 11,000 people spread across nine atolls. Its total land area is 26 square kilometers. Its average elevation is two meters above sea level. The ocean is rising at approximately 3.9 millimeters per year in this part of the Pacific — faster than the global average. Tuvalu's government has negotiated agreements with Australia and New Zealand to relocate its population. It is also arguing before international legal bodies that it should remain a sovereign state even if all its land disappears beneath the water. This has never happened before in the history of nations.

The camera descends slowly, the lagoon on one side, the open Pacific on the other, the strip of land barely visible between them.

SUBJECT: Daily life on an island where the ocean is always adjacent

NARRATION BEAT: There is nowhere on this island that is far from the water. On a calm day that is beautiful. On a high tide day, the water comes in under the doors.

Scene 3 — The King Tide

A street in Funafuti, during a king tide event. Water moves across the road — not flooding in the dramatic sense, just present, a few centimeters deep, spreading across the pavement, surrounding the base of buildings. Residents walk through it. A child stands in the water and watches it come. The camera holds in a static wide shot as the water covers the road.

SETTING: Funafuti, during a king tide — periodic extreme high tide event
SUBJECT: Seawater moving across the streets of the capital
IN-SCENE MOTION: Water spreads slowly across the road from right to left; a child stands still watching; residents step around it
LIGHTING: Overcast midday; flat light; the water reflects the gray sky
NARRATION BEAT: King tides happen several times a year. The water doesn't rush in. It seeps up from below — through the coral, through the sand, through the road itself. The atoll is so thin that the water table is connected to the ocean.

Scene 4 — The Agreement

A formal meeting room — ministers at a long table, documents in front of them. The Falepili Union agreement between Tuvalu and Australia, signed 2023. Two men sign the document. Cameras flash. Hands are shaken. The camera holds in a wide shot on the room, the flags of both countries visible.

SETTING: A diplomatic signing ceremony, 2023 — the Falepili Union
SUBJECT: The formal agreement that gives Tuvaluans the right to migrate to Australia
IN-SCENE MOTION: Pens move across paper; cameras flash; hands meet and separate
LIGHTING: Press conference lighting — bright, flat; the flags are the brightest colors in the room
NARRATION BEAT: The Falepili Union gives Tuvalu's citizens the right to live and work in Australia and provides Australian security guarantees to the islands. In exchange, Tuvalu agreed to consult Australia on foreign relations. Critics called it a sovereignty trade. Supporters called it survival.

A school classroom in Funafuti. Children sit at desks. A teacher stands at a whiteboard — a map of the Pacific is drawn on it. She traces the outline of Tuvalu with her finger, then the outline of Australia, then New Zealand. The children watch. The camera holds in a medium shot on the teacher and the whiteboard.

SETTING: A primary school classroom, Funafuti
SUBJECT: A teacher explaining Tuvalu's geography and situation
IN-SCENE MOTION: The teacher's hand moves across the whiteboard map; a child in the front row raises her hand
LIGHTING: Natural light from windows on one side; the whiteboard is in the brighter half of the room
NARRATION BEAT: These children are being taught about climate change as a fact of their immediate lives, not a distant possibility. The lesson plan includes what to do if the family has to leave.

Scene 6 — The Legal Argument

A formal hall — an international tribunal, or a hearing room at the United Nations in New York. A Tuvaluan lawyer or official stands at a podium, speaking. The camera holds in a steady medium shot, the speaker in the center of the frame.

SETTING: A United Nations or international legal forum, present day
SUBJECT: A Tuvaluan representative making the case for continued statehood after potential submersion
IN-SCENE MOTION: The speaker gestures; papers are shuffled at the table behind him; a translator whispers into a headset
LIGHTING: Formal institutional lighting; the podium is lit from above; the audience seats are darker
NARRATION BEAT: International law has never faced this question: can a state continue to exist after its territory disappears? Tuvalu is arguing yes — that statehood, once established, does not depend on land. No court has ruled on this. No precedent exists.

Scene 7 — The View Across the Lagoon

Standing at the edge of the lagoon in the late afternoon. The water is completely calm, reflecting the pink and orange of the sky. On the far side of the lagoon, the strip of land is just visible. The camera holds in a static wide shot as the light changes.

SETTING: The Funafuti lagoon, late afternoon
SUBJECT: The lagoon at its most beautiful — the landscape that is disappearing

NARRATION BEAT: This is what there is to lose. Not just territory on a map, but this — this specific light on this specific water, with the strip of land that people have lived on for 3,000 years still visible on the other side.

Scene 8 — The Ocean, at Night

The open Pacific, seen from the shore of Funafuti at night. Stars are visible — the Milky Way crossing the sky. The ocean is dark. Waves break on the shore, steady and quiet. The camera holds in a static wide shot looking out to sea.

SETTING: The shore of Funafuti, at night
SUBJECT: The ocean — the force that defines Tuvalu's existence and threatens it
IN-SCENE MOTION: Waves break and pull back; the stars are still; the camera holds
LIGHTING: Starlight and a low moon; the breaking waves are the only white in the frame
NARRATION BEAT: The ocean made Tuvalu possible. The Polynesian navigators who settled these islands did it by reading the sea — currents, stars, wave patterns. The same ocean, responding to a different kind of human knowledge, is now reading Tuvalu back.

Key Takeaway
Tuvalu is the first place where the question "what happens when a country disappears?" has become a real and urgent administrative problem. The answer being worked out right now — in diplomatic agreements, in international courts, in classroom lesson plans, and in the families deciding whether to leave — will set precedent for every low-lying nation that follows. Tuvalu is not a metaphor for climate change. It is the place where climate change runs out of metaphors and becomes logistics.
