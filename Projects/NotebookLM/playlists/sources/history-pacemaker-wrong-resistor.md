# The Wrong Resistor: How the Pacemaker Was Invented by Mistake

In 1956, an engineer building a heart rhythm recorder grabbed the wrong part from a box. The circuit it produced pulsed at roughly the rate of a human heartbeat. Wilson Greatbatch stared at it and understood immediately what he had made — something no one had asked him to make, and something that would eventually keep hundreds of thousands of people alive every year.

---

Scene 1 — What He Was Building

Wilson Greatbatch was working at the University of Buffalo in 1956, helping build a device to record heartbeats for medical research. The goal was straightforward: capture the electrical signal of the heart so doctors could study it. He was not trying to fix hearts. He was trying to listen to them.

Scene 2 — The Wrong Box

While assembling the oscillator circuit for the recorder, Greatbatch reached into his parts box and pulled out a resistor. He needed a 10,000-ohm resistor. He grabbed a 1-megohm resistor — one hundred times the resistance he needed. He installed it without noticing and switched the circuit on.

Instead of recording a signal, the circuit started producing one. A rhythmic pulse, on and off, roughly 1.8 milliseconds apart. Close to the rate of a human heartbeat.

Scene 3 — The Moment

Greatbatch later described what happened next in plain terms: he stared at it for a while and then realized he was looking at something that could drive a human heart. The circuit was doing what a damaged sinoatrial node — the heart's natural pacemaker — could no longer do reliably. It was producing the timing signal that kept the muscle beating in rhythm.

He knew enough about cardiac medicine to understand what that meant. People were dying from heart block, a condition where the electrical signal that coordinates the heartbeat breaks down. There was no reliable treatment. He had just accidentally built one.

Scene 4 — Two Years in a Barn

Greatbatch spent the next two years in a small workshop behind his house, building a version small enough to implant inside a human body. The engineering problems were not trivial — battery life, biocompatibility, size, reliability. He solved them one by one. His employer at the time had no interest in the project. He worked on it in the evenings and on weekends.

Scene 5 — The First Implant

In 1960, the first fully implantable pacemaker was placed in a human patient. The patient lived for 18 more months before dying of an unrelated cause. The device worked the entire time.

By the end of the decade, pacemakers were saving thousands of lives a year. By the time Greatbatch died in 2011, more than 600,000 were being implanted annually worldwide.

Scene 6 — What One Mistake Made

The wrong resistor cost a few cents. Greatbatch kept it. He later said it was the single most important mistake of his life, and that he had spent a long time thinking about what it meant that the error was the discovery — not the correction of the error, not the careful research that followed, but the moment the wrong part went into the wrong slot and produced exactly the right result.

---

Key Takeaway

Wilson Greatbatch invented the implantable pacemaker because he grabbed the wrong resistor from a parts box. The mistake produced a circuit that pulsed like a heartbeat, and he recognized it immediately for what it was. The device he built in a barn over the next two years has kept millions of hearts beating. The resistor that started it cost less than a dollar.
