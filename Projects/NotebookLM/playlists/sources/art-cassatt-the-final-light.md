# Mary Cassatt: The Final Light

She went blind from cataracts in her 60s. The last twelve years of her life were silence — an artist defined by her ability to see, without that ability. The instrument she had spent a lifetime building, gone.

---

Scene 1 — The Studio Goes Dark

Cassatt stands in her studio at the Château de Beaufresne, north of Paris. She is in her late 60s. She moves toward a canvas on an easel, picks up a brush, and brings it close to the surface. She stops. She leans in. Steps back. Sets the brush down. The camera watches from across the room, still. Light through the tall studio windows is strong and clear. She seems to be standing in it but not seeing it.

SETTING: Cassatt's studio at Château de Beaufresne, c. 1911–1914
SUBJECT: Cassatt losing her sight in her studio
IN-SCENE MOTION: She approaches the canvas, raises the brush, then sets it down; her eyes search the surface
LIGHTING: Strong afternoon light from studio windows — bright and ironic given what is failing
NARRATION BEAT: By her late 60s, cataracts were destroying Cassatt's sight. The eye she had spent sixty years training was failing. She stopped painting in 1914. She would live another twelve years.

---

Scene 2 — The Japanese Prints, 1890

A print studio, Paris, 1890. Cassatt stands before a printmaking press, pulling a fresh sheet from the roller. She lifts it carefully and holds it up to the light — a mother and child in flat color, Japanese-influenced, no shadow, pure outline. She studies the print closely. Behind her, earlier sheets hang drying on a line. The camera tilts slowly upward along the hanging prints, then holds.

SETTING: A Paris printmaking studio, 1890
SUBJECT: Cassatt reviewing a print from her Japanese-influenced aquatint series
IN-SCENE MOTION: She lifts the print and holds it to the light; behind her, hanging prints sway slightly
LIGHTING: Cool studio light; the lifted print catches more light as she holds it higher
NARRATION BEAT: The 1890 Japanese woodblock print exhibition changed how Cassatt thought about flatness, line, and color. Her response — a suite of color aquatints made over the following year — is now considered among the finest prints in American art history.

---

Scene 3 — The Opinion That Didn't Change

A Paris café, early 1900s. Cassatt sits across from an art dealer, listening. He slides documents across the table toward her — offers, appraisals, an exhibition proposal. She does not look at them. She looks at him. He waits. She says something short and direct. He takes the papers back.

SETTING: A Paris café, early 1900s
SUBJECT: Cassatt dealing with the art world on her own terms
IN-SCENE MOTION: Papers slide across the table; she speaks; he retrieves them
LIGHTING: Café midday light — bright from front windows, deeper shadow toward the rear
NARRATION BEAT: Cassatt was not easy. She refused work that didn't interest her, declined exhibitions that compromised her positioning, and said exactly what she thought about other painters. She was this way at 30 and at 70.

---

Scene 4 — The Long Silence

The studio at Beaufresne, now. No canvas on the easel. The windows are bright. A chair faces the window, and an elderly Cassatt sits in it, hands in her lap, facing the light she can no longer clearly see. The camera is outside the window, looking in through old glass that slightly blurs the image. She does not move. The room behind her is still.

SETTING: Château de Beaufresne, 1914–1926
SUBJECT: Cassatt in the years after she stopped painting
IN-SCENE MOTION: Light slowly shifts across the floor as clouds move; she is entirely still
LIGHTING: Diffuse outside light through old window glass; slightly softened, slightly blue
NARRATION BEAT: She sat near windows. Not working — she could not work — but near the light. She lived this way for twelve years, until 1926.

---

Scene 5 — What She Left

The Metropolitan Museum, New York. Morning, before the crowds arrive. A guard moves slowly through the Impressionist gallery. He passes a Degas, a Monet, a Pissarro. He stops briefly before a Cassatt — a mother looking down at a child, who looks up at her — then moves on. The camera stays. The gallery is empty. Light from a skylight crosses the floor in a long diagonal.

SETTING: The Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York, early morning
SUBJECT: A Cassatt in the permanent collection
IN-SCENE MOTION: The guard walks past and exits the frame; the camera holds; light from the skylight shifts imperceptibly
LIGHTING: Museum morning light — diffuse, even; a shaft from the overhead skylight crossing the floor
NARRATION BEAT: At the Met, the Art Institute, the National Gallery — what is there came partly through her. The collection she built for others outlasted the sight she built it with.
