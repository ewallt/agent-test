# Columbus's Wrong Calculation: How an Error Discovered a Continent

Christopher Columbus sailed west from the Canary Islands on September 6, 1492, believing he was roughly 2,400 miles from Japan. He was off by about 8,000 miles. He had made a series of compounding mathematical errors, misread a key ancient text, and used a unit of measurement incorrectly. If he had done the calculation correctly, he would never have sailed at all.

## The Calculation

The central question Columbus needed to answer before proposing his voyage was: how far is it from Europe to Asia going west? The answer to that question had been worked out with reasonable accuracy by the ancient Greek mathematician Eratosthenes in the third century BC. Eratosthenes calculated the circumference of the Earth at approximately 25,000 miles — a figure remarkably close to the actual value of 24,901 miles.

Columbus rejected this calculation. He used instead the figure proposed by the Arab geographer al-Farghani, who had calculated a smaller Earth. But Columbus made a critical error: al-Farghani had expressed the Earth's circumference in Arabic miles, which are longer than the Roman miles Columbus used for the conversion. Columbus converted Arabic miles to Roman miles without adjusting for the difference, shrinking the Earth by roughly a quarter.

He also relied on the account of Marco Polo, who described Asia as extending much farther east than it actually does. Columbus added Marco Polo's inflated estimate of Asia's eastern extension onto his already-shrunken globe. The result was a journey that looked, on paper, navigable.

His estimate: roughly 2,400 miles from the Canaries to Japan. The actual distance, across open ocean with no landmass in the way, is approximately 10,600 miles.

## The Expert Rejection

Columbus presented his proposal to the Portuguese crown in the early 1480s and was rejected. The Portuguese had court astronomers and geographers who understood the actual size of the Earth. Their objection was not that Columbus was proposing something too bold — it was that he was proposing something impossible. The distance was too great. Ships of the period could not carry enough food and water for a journey of that length.

They were correct. Columbus's plan, if executed as he understood it, could not have succeeded. He would have run out of supplies somewhere in the middle of the Pacific Ocean and died.

Columbus took his proposal to the Spanish monarchs Ferdinand and Isabella. They convened a commission of experts who reached the same conclusion as the Portuguese: the distance was too great. They rejected him twice. He was in the process of being finally rejected for a third time when, in January 1492, the fall of Granada completed the Reconquista and put the Spanish monarchs in an expansive mood. They reversed the decision, funded the voyage, and Columbus sailed in August.

## The Crossing

Columbus's crew grew mutinous after five weeks at sea with no land in sight. They were far beyond where Columbus had promised land would appear. On October 10th, two days before landfall, the situation was close to open revolt. Columbus negotiated: three more days. On October 12th, land appeared — not Japan, but a small island in what is now the Bahamas.

Columbus never stopped believing he had reached Asia. He called the inhabitants "Indians." He made four voyages to the Caribbean and the Central American coast. He died in 1506 still maintaining that he had found a western route to the Indies. The navigators who followed him understood what he had found; he did not.

## The Irony

The actual distance from the Canary Islands to the American coastline is approximately 2,500 miles — almost exactly what Columbus had calculated for Japan. He had predicted a journey of a certain length. The journey was that length. But there was no Asia at the end of it. There was a continent that no European cosmography had accounted for, sitting in the middle of what Columbus had assumed was open ocean.

The Americas were not on any map. They were not in any calculation. They were, from the perspective of Columbus's geography, simply not there. And then they were.

## What the Error Made Possible

Columbus's wrong math had three consequences that would not have followed from correct math. First, it made the voyage seem possible when it was not — which is why he could convince himself and eventually his patrons that it could be done. Second, it got him across the ocean on the strength of his conviction before the crew could stop him. Third, it put him in the right place: a ship aiming for Japan and finding nothing would turn back; a ship that found something at the expected distance had reason to keep going.

The error was the precondition for the discovery. A Columbus who knew the correct distance between Europe and Asia would have understood that the journey was beyond the technology of his era. He would have been right. He would have stayed home. The Americas would have waited for someone else.
