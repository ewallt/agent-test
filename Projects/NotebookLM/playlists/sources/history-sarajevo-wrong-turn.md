# The Wrong Turn at Sarajevo

On June 28, 1914, Archduke Franz Ferdinand of Austria-Hungary and his wife Sophie were assassinated in Sarajevo — an event that triggered a chain of mobilizations, ultimatums, and declarations that killed twenty million people. History tends to frame this as inevitable: the powder keg was primed, the spark was coming. What history often omits is how completely the assassination almost failed — and that it succeeded only because of a wrong turn.

## The First Attempt Fails

The Black Hand, a Serbian nationalist organization, had stationed seven assassins along the Appel Quay, the road the Archduke's motorcade would travel. Each was assigned a position. Each carried a pistol or a grenade.

The first to act was Nedeljko Čabrinović. As the open car passed, he threw a grenade. The driver saw it coming, accelerated, and the grenade bounced off the folded-back convertible roof and detonated under the following car. Two officers were wounded. Franz Ferdinand was unhurt.

The motorcade sped to the town hall. The remaining five assassins, including nineteen-year-old Gavrilo Princip, stood along the route and did nothing. Some lost their nerve. Some were blocked by the crowd. The motorcade passed through them all. At the town hall, Franz Ferdinand was furious. He had come to demonstrate Habsburg goodwill toward his Slavic subjects. Instead, someone had tried to blow him up.

## The Route Change

After the town hall reception, General Potiorek — the Austrian military governor — suggested a change of plans: the Archduke would visit the officers wounded in the grenade attack at the hospital. A different route was agreed upon. The drivers were informed.

Or most of them were.

The lead driver, Leopold Lojka, had not received the updated instructions. He turned onto Franz Josef Street — the original route — out of habit, or confusion, or simply because no one had clearly told him otherwise.

Potiorek, in the car, realized the error and told Lojka to stop. Lojka braked. He shifted into reverse to back out and turn around.

The engine stalled.

## The Coincidence

Gavrilo Princip had given up. After watching the motorcade disappear toward the town hall without acting, he had wandered off the Appel Quay and drifted into Franz Josef Street. He stopped in front of Schiller's Delicatessen — some accounts say to buy a sandwich, others simply that he was standing there, having abandoned any hope the mission would succeed.

The Archduke's car rolled to a halt approximately five feet in front of him.

Princip drew his pistol and fired twice. The first bullet struck Franz Ferdinand in the jugular vein. The second struck Sophie in the abdomen. Both died within the hour.

## The Mechanism

What followed was not automatic, but it moved with terrible speed. Austria-Hungary blamed Serbia and issued a deliberately unacceptable ultimatum. Serbia's partial compliance was rejected. Austria declared war. Russia mobilized in support of Serbia. Germany declared war on Russia and then France. Germany invaded Belgium. Britain declared war on Germany.

In thirty-seven days, every major European power was at war. The sequence was not inevitable — at several points, different decisions might have halted it — but the spark was Sarajevo, and Sarajevo was Lojka's wrong turn.

## The Deeper Structure

The assassination is often taught as evidence that great historical forces produce great historical events — that the nationalism, militarism, and imperial rivalry of 1914 were so combustible that some incident would have ignited them. This may be true. But the specific incident — the one that actually started the war — depended on a wrong turn, a stalled engine, and an assassin standing in exactly the right place at exactly the wrong moment.

The question this raises is not whether the war was inevitable in some abstract sense. It is whether we understand what we mean when we call anything inevitable. If a single driver following the wrong instructions produced the most destructive event in European history to that point, then historical forces are not forces in the way that gravity is a force. They are tendencies — directional pressures that still require a specific, contingent, recoverable moment to become catastrophe.

The wrong turn at Sarajevo did not cause World War One in any simple sense. But it did cause the assassination that caused the war. And the assassination was, by the testimony of everyone involved — the assassins themselves, the security officials, the surviving passengers — a matter of seconds and inches.

History is full of near misses that missed. Sarajevo is the near miss that didn't.

## Key Facts

- Date: June 28, 1914
- Location: Sarajevo, Bosnia (then part of Austria-Hungary)
- Assassin: Gavrilo Princip, age 19, member of the Black Hand
- Victims: Archduke Franz Ferdinand and Duchess Sophie
- The wrong turn: Driver Leopold Lojka turned onto Franz Josef Street instead of the hospital route
- Time between stall and shooting: seconds
- Days from assassination to general European war: 37
- Deaths in World War One: approximately 20 million
