# The Licked Finger: How a Sweetener Was Discovered by Accident

James Schlatter was a chemist at G.D. Searle & Company in 1965 working on a drug to treat gastric ulcers. He was synthesizing a compound called aspartyl-phenylalanine methyl ester — a dipeptide fragment in a longer drug candidate. He spilled some on his hand, and later, reaching for a piece of paper, licked his finger to get a better grip. He tasted something intensely sweet. He had not been looking for a sweetener. He had been trying to treat ulcers. One licked finger redirected the global sugar industry.

---

Scene 1 — The Drug Program

G.D. Searle & Company, Skokie, Illinois, 1965. A pharmaceutical research laboratory. James Schlatter is part of a team trying to synthesize a drug that might treat gastric ulcers by blocking the hormone gastrin. The compound he is working on — aspartyl-phenylalanine methyl ester — is an intermediate step in a longer synthesis, not a target in itself. The camera tracks slowly along the bench, past the glassware and reagent bottles, past the written synthesis route, settling on Schlatter's hands at work — careful, routine, nothing suggesting that this particular compound on this particular afternoon is different from any other.

Scene 2 — The Spill

The same laboratory, a specific afternoon in 1965. Schlatter is transferring the compound and some spills onto his hand. He wipes it off and continues. The camera holds on his hand for a moment — the smear of compound, the wipe, the return to work — before cutting back to the wider bench. The spill is not an event. It is the kind of minor contamination that happens dozens of times a day in a working laboratory. Nothing about the scene suggests consequence.

Scene 3 — The Licked Finger

An hour or two later. Schlatter reaches for a piece of paper on the bench. He licks his finger to get purchase on it — a gesture so automatic that he is already reaching for the paper before the taste registers. He stops. He tastes something intensely, unexpectedly sweet. He looks at his hand. He looks at the bench. He thinks backward through the afternoon to the spill, to the compound, to the synthesis. The camera pushes slowly in on his face as the calculation runs — not alarm, not excitement yet, but the specific stillness of a chemist who has tasted something that should not be sweet.

Scene 4 — The Verification

Searle laboratories, the days after the discovery. Schlatter is systematic: he confirms that the sweetness came from the compound and not a contaminant, tests it against known references, establishes the concentration at which it is effective. Aspartame is approximately 200 times sweeter than sucrose. It has no caloric value at the concentrations needed to produce sweetness. He files a report. The camera tracks slowly along the lab notebook — the careful entries, the comparison tests, the concentration measurements — as the accidental discovery becomes documented science, one methodical step at a time.

Scene 5 — The Long Road to Approval

Searle's regulatory offices and Washington D.C., 1965–1981. Sixteen years of FDA review, safety studies, political controversy. The compound is approved for dry foods in 1974, then approval is suspended, then restored. The camera rises slowly above a conference table covered in documents — safety filings, study results, regulatory correspondence — to show the full scale of the paper record required to bring a single accidentally-discovered compound to market. By the time aspartame is fully approved for beverages in 1983, Schlatter's licked finger is nearly two decades in the past.

Scene 6 — The Blue Packet

A diner, any year after 1983. A table with a sugar caddy: white packets, pink packets, blue packets. Equal, NutraSweet, then all the store brands that followed. Diet sodas, sugar-free gum, low-calorie yogurt, thousands of products in dozens of countries. The compound in the blue packet is the compound Schlatter spilled on his hand in 1965 while trying to treat gastric ulcers. The camera moves slowly from the blue packet to the coffee cup to the window — the ordinary geometry of a diner table — as if the accidental chemistry were just another feature of the room.

---

Key Takeaway

James Schlatter was not looking for a sweetener. He was synthesizing an intermediate compound in an ulcer drug program when he spilled some on his hand, licked his finger later, and tasted something intensely sweet. The discovery of aspartame — one of the most widely used artificial sweeteners in the world — required no insight, no hypothesis, and no intention. It required a spill, a reflex, and a chemist who paid attention to what his tongue was telling him.
