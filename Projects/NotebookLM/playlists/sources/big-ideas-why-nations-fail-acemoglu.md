# Why Nations Fail: The Origins of Power, Prosperity, and Poverty

Daron Acemoglu and James Robinson are economists who spent fifteen years trying to answer one question: why are some countries rich and others poor? Not poor because of climate, not poor because of culture, not poor because they lack resources. Poor because of the rules — who makes them, who benefits from them, and who gets shut out. Their 2012 book is an argument that institutions, not geography, not culture, not luck, explain the gap.

---

Scene 1 — The Fence

Nogales, Arizona and Nogales, Sonora. The same city, divided by a fence. The same desert climate. The same ethnic population on both sides. The same culture, more or less, descending from the same Spanish colonial history. On the north side: average household income around $30,000 a year, high school graduation rates above 80 percent, reliable electricity, working courts, property rights that mean something. On the south side: income a fraction of that, public services unreliable, rule of law uncertain. The fence runs through the middle of what was once a single town. The camera rises above the fence line in a slow crane shot, the two cities visible below — same landscape, same people, different outcomes — until the fence itself disappears into the desert in both directions and the only visible difference between the two sides is what was built there.

Scene 2 — The Encomienda

Mexico, 1520. A Spanish colonial administrator is distributing grants — encomiendas — that give conquistadors legal control over indigenous labor in specific territories. The system is not slavery exactly, but it functions the same way: the indigenous population works; the grantee receives the product. The Spanish Crown wants extraction — silver, gold, agricultural surplus — and designed its colonial institutions to deliver exactly that. These institutions will persist in modified form for three hundred years, and their successor institutions will persist for another two hundred years after that. The camera tracks slowly along a line of men being registered into the labor system — names recorded, obligations assigned — and holds on the administrator's hand moving across the ledger page, writing the rules that will determine what these men's great-great-grandchildren can own.

Scene 3 — The Black Death Divergence

Europe, 1350. The plague has killed a third of the continent's population. Labor is suddenly scarce. In Western Europe — England, France, the Low Countries — landlords who try to maintain the old feudal labor obligations find their serfs walking off to offer their services elsewhere. Wages rise. Serfdom quietly collapses because the balance of power has shifted. In Eastern Europe — Poland, Russia, Prussia — landlords respond the same way a monopolist responds to a threat: by closing ranks, passing laws, using state power to keep peasants tied to the land. The same event, the same disease, hitting both regions simultaneously, and the outcomes diverge. The camera holds on a crossroads sign at the edge of a German village — one road going west, one going east — as a figure with a cart pauses to decide, while behind him a church bell rings the year.

Scene 4 — Venice Closes Its Doors

Venice, 1315. The serrata — the closing. The city's merchant republic has been, for two centuries, the most economically dynamic place in Europe. Anyone with capital and ambition could enter commerce through the commenda contract — a legal arrangement that let a poor man partner with a rich man to finance a voyage, split the profits, and build wealth from nothing. In 1315, the existing merchant families vote to close the Great Council to new members. The commenda fades. Entry is now restricted to those already inside. The camera pushes slowly in on the doorway of the Doge's Palace — a beautiful arch, two guards — as a merchant approaches with papers, is turned back, and walks away down the canal. Venice will be a great city for another century on the strength of what it built. Then it will not be.

Scene 5 — The Glorious Revolution

London, 1688. Parliament has just removed King James II and invited William of Orange to take the throne on their terms. The terms matter more than the throne. Parliament will control taxation. Parliament will control spending. Property rights will be enforceable against the Crown. Courts will be independent. This is not democracy — most people still cannot vote — but it is the moment when the English state stops being a tool of royal extraction and starts being a tool that commercial interests can use to protect their own investments. The camera moves through the chamber of the House of Commons during the vote — handheld, slightly unstable, figures in wigs debating quietly — and settles for a moment on the tally being recorded, the pen scratching the numbers that will, over the next century, produce the Industrial Revolution.

Scene 6 — The Resource Curse

The Niger Delta, present day. One of the largest oil deposits in Africa, under one of the poorest populations in the world. The oil has been there for sixty years. The money from the oil has moved — to state-owned corporations, to officials, to a network of extraction that looks, structurally, like a Spanish encomienda with better accounting. The people who live above the deposit do not own it and cannot negotiate with those who do. The camera rises in a slow pull-back from a village without reliable electricity, past the flares burning off excess gas from the nearby rigs, through the orange light they cast on the water, until the flares are small below and the delta stretches to the sea — wealth measured in billions, distributed in a way that makes the village possible and necessary.

---

Key Takeaway

Acemoglu and Robinson's argument is that inclusive institutions — secure property rights, rule of law, open political participation — produce growth because they let the broadest population take economic risks and keep the returns, while extractive institutions concentrate both political power and economic gains in a small group. The gap between Nogales, Arizona and Nogales, Sonora is not about geography or culture. It's about which side of the fence got which set of rules.
