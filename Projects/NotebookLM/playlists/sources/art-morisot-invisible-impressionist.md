# Berthe Morisot: The Invisible Impressionist

When the first Impressionist exhibition opened in April 1874 — the show that gave the movement its name — one of the participating artists was a thirty-two-year-old woman named Berthe Morisot. She exhibited in seven of the eight Impressionist exhibitions (all but one, when she had just given birth). She was a central figure in the circle, not a peripheral one — a peer of Monet, Renoir, and Degas, respected by all of them. Édouard Manet, the patriarch of the group, considered her his most important artistic interlocutor for the last decade of his life.

She has spent most of the century since being described as "Manet's sister-in-law."

## Origins and Training

Morisot was born in 1841 into a wealthy, cultivated bourgeois family. She and her sister Edma received serious artistic training — unusual for women of their class, who were expected to treat painting as an accomplishment rather than a vocation. Their teacher, the landscape painter Camille Corot, recognized Berthe as exceptional and pushed her toward the Louvre, toward direct observation, toward the kind of rigor that Corot himself practiced.

Edma eventually married, stopped painting, and later said she regretted it for the rest of her life. Berthe did not stop. She continued working through marriage (she married Eugène Manet, Édouard's brother, in 1874), through the birth of her daughter Julie, through the deaths of her husband and brother, until her own death in 1895.

## What She Painted

Morisot's subjects were the spaces women occupied in nineteenth-century bourgeois life: the garden, the nursery, the parlor, the balcony overlooking the street below. Critics sometimes used this as a diminishment — she painted small things, domestic things. The criticism misses the point entirely.

She was painting the world she had access to with the same analytical attention Degas brought to the ballet or Monet brought to the Seine. The domestic space was not a limitation she accepted reluctantly; it was a subject she interrogated. Her paintings of women at windows — looking out at a world they could observe but not freely move through — carry a charge that is hard to read as accidental.

Her technique was aggressive. She worked with rapid, loose strokes that left large areas of canvas barely covered, figures suggested rather than fully rendered. Critics who disliked her work called it unfinished. What they were seeing was a deliberate formal choice: she was less interested in completing the illusion than in capturing the quality of a moment — the light on a face, the energy of a child in motion. In this she was more radical than most of her male contemporaries.

## The Manet Problem

Morisot's relationship with Édouard Manet is one of the most written-about in art history, and most of the writing gets it wrong. He painted her eleven times, more than any other model. The paintings are charged. Speculation about the nature of their relationship has never stopped.

What is clear: they influenced each other. She pushed him toward looser brushwork and lighter palette. He pushed her toward ambition and seriousness at moments when the social pressures on women artists might have led her to retreat. When she married his brother, the friendship continued unchanged.

The problem is that in most accounts, Manet's name appears first and hers second — as if she were the satellite of his genius rather than a talent in her own right. The paintings argue otherwise.

## The Recovery

For most of the twentieth century, Morisot was a footnote. Her work was collected but not studied; exhibited but not examined. The feminist art history movement of the 1970s began to change this, situating her not as an anomaly but as evidence of a larger pattern: that women artists had always been present, had always been significant, and had been systematically written out of the story.

The reassessment is still incomplete. Major retrospectives in the 1980s and 1990s established her importance in academic circles, but the popular image of Impressionism — Monet's garden, Renoir's lunches, the sun-drenched leisure of a mostly male group — has been slower to make room for her.

She was there at the beginning. She was there for almost all of it. The paintings are abundant, distinctive, and unmistakable once you know what to look for.
