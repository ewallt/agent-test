# The Ship That Wasn't Built Yet: Morgan Robertson's Titan

In 1898, a former merchant sailor published a short novel about the sinking of the world's largest ocean liner. The ship in the novel was called the Titan. Fourteen years later, a real ship called the Titanic went down in nearly identical circumstances. Robertson had been dead for three years by the time anyone fully mapped the comparison.

---

Scene 1 — The Man Who Knew Ships

Morgan Robertson spent years at sea before he started writing. He worked his way up from cabin boy to first mate on merchant vessels, which meant he understood ships the way a carpenter understands wood — from the inside, not from a brochure. When he turned to fiction in the 1880s and 1890s, most of what he wrote was sea stories. He knew the technology, the culture, and the engineering. He was not a mystic. He was a sailor who had spent a long time thinking about what could go wrong.

Scene 2 — The Novel

In 1898 Robertson published a novella called *Futility, or the Wreck of the Titan*. The setup: the Titan is the largest ship ever built, declared unsinkable by its designers, crossing the North Atlantic in April at high speed. It strikes an iceberg. It sinks. Most passengers die.

The novel sold modestly, drew little attention, and was largely forgotten within a few years.

Scene 3 — The Numbers

The Titanic sailed in April 1912. It was the largest ship ever built, declared unsinkable by its designers, crossing the North Atlantic at high speed. It struck an iceberg. It sank. Most passengers died.

The specific overlaps are not vague. Robertson's Titan was 800 feet long; the Titanic was 882. Robertson gave the Titan a top speed of 25 knots; the Titanic's was 24. Both ships were described in nearly identical language as monuments to human engineering. Both were traveling in the same direction, in the same month, in the same ocean.

Scene 4 — The Lifeboats

Both ships also had a fatal shortage of lifeboats. Robertson made this a central plot point — passengers died in the novel because there were not enough boats to go around. He included it because he knew why it happened: ship owners cut lifeboats to free up deck space, and passengers preferred the open deck to the sight of emergency equipment. It was an economic decision dressed as an aesthetic one.

In 1912, the lifeboat shortage on the Titanic became the central scandal of the real disaster. The reasoning was identical to what Robertson had written fourteen years earlier.

Scene 5 — What Robertson Said

After the Titanic sank, reporters tracked Robertson down and asked the obvious question. He declined to claim psychic ability. He said he had studied ships, understood the physics and the economics, and written down what he thought was likely to happen. The lifeboat shortage was not a prophecy — it was a predictable consequence of how the industry was operating.

He may have been right about that. It does not explain the ship's name, the month, the speed, or the length.

Scene 6 — The Reprint

*Futility* was reprinted in 1912 after the Titanic sank. Robertson died in 1915. He never fully explained the coincidence. He said once that he just knew ships.

The novel is still in print.

---

Key Takeaway

Fourteen years before the Titanic sank, a sailor-turned-novelist described an almost identical disaster in specific detail — the ship's name, size, speed, month, ocean, and fatal lifeboat shortage. Robertson said he was just extrapolating from how the industry worked. The lifeboat reasoning holds up. The rest of the numbers are harder to explain.
