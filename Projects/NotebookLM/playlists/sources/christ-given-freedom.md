# Christ-Given Freedom: Galatians 5:1

E.J. Waggoner's study of Galatians 5:1 opens not with an argument but with a scene — a woman in a synagogue, bent double for eighteen years. Everything that follows is an unpacking of what happened to her, and what it means for every person since.

---

Scene 1 — Eighteen Years

In a synagogue on the Sabbath, a woman moves through the room bent forward under a weight she has carried for eighteen years. She cannot lift herself. She does not stand straight. She is there, present in the place of worship, but unable to raise her face toward it — held down by something she has no power to remove.

Waggoner places this image at the center of his study deliberately. Not an abstraction. A woman. A body. Eighteen years of accumulated weight. And then Jesus sees her across the room and calls her to Him.

---

Scene 2 — The Condition Described

The woman's situation, Waggoner argues, is a precise description of every person before they meet Christ. Two points: she was bound by something she did not choose and could not escape, and she had no strength of her own to break free. Paul calls this being "without strength" — translated from the same word used for the woman's infirmity. Without strength means no strength at all. Not weakened, not struggling — without.

Sin is the cord that binds. The person under its weight is not someone who prefers to stay bent. They are someone who cannot, by any effort, stand upright.

---

Scene 3 — The Exchange

Jesus crosses the room and lays His hands on her. That touch is not incidental — it is the whole of the Gospel in a gesture. He enters the condition He finds. He was born under the law, to redeem those under it. He was made sin, He who knew no sin, so that in Him we might be made the righteousness of God.

The exchange is total. He becomes what we are so that we can become what He is. The strength He gives is not borrowed — it is His own, transferred. The bent woman did not leave the synagogue with a crutch. She left standing straight.

---

Scene 4 — Why the Freedom

Waggoner pauses on the purpose. The woman was not freed so she could choose to remain bent. She was freed so she could walk in the life her body was made for.

Freedom from sin has the same shape. Before Christ, the law stood as accuser — a heavy iron door locked from the outside. In Christ, its demand is met. He puts righteousness not only on us but in us, so the law has no charge. The heavy door swings open. What was the accuser becomes the witness. The door opens not because the law was abolished, but because the sentence was served and the debt paid in full.

---

Scene 5 — The Word Spoken While She Was Still Bent

This is the pivot. Jesus speaks before anything changes in the visible world. "Woman, thou art loosed from thine infirmity." Present tense. She is still bent when He says it. The freedom is declared before the body straightens. The word comes first — and then, immediately, she is straight.

Waggoner's point is that this is exactly how He speaks to every captive. Not "thou shalt be loosed," not "thou mayest be loosed if thou art worthy." Present tense. The proclamation has already gone out to every soul bowed down under the weight of sin. The question is only whether they will hear it.

---

Scene 6 — Faith Lays Hold of What Is Already There

Faith does not make things true. It lays hold of what is already true. If the freedom were not already given, there would be nothing for faith to grasp — we cannot believe what is not so.

The woman did not produce her own healing by resolving to believe. She simply lifted her head because He had already spoken. Our part is not to argue with the Lord. Hold fast to the word in the face of every doubt. The word that declares freedom is also the word that maintains it.

---

Scene 7 — The Law of Liberty

At the end of the account, the woman glorifies God. She does not leave at a slightly lesser angle. She walks away straight — free, moving in the life she was made for.

Waggoner closes here: the law of God is the shape of that life. In Christ, the law that once imprisoned becomes the law of liberty — no longer a jailer but a description of the upright walk. "Stand fast therefore in the liberty wherewith Christ hath made us free." Not a call to willpower. A call to stay in the posture the word produced.

---

Key Takeaway

In the synagogue, a woman who had carried eighteen years of weight straightened the moment a word was spoken over her. The word came before the change. Waggoner's point is that the same word has been spoken over every captive. The freedom is not something to earn or reach toward. It has already been declared. Faith is simply the act of standing up.
