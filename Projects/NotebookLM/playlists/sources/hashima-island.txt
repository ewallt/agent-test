Hashima Island — The City That Left Overnight

Hashima Island sits nine miles off the coast of Nagasaki, Japan. It is small — 480 meters long, 160 meters wide — and from the sea it looks like a battleship: a long concrete hull riding low in the water, surrounded by a seawall, bristling with towers. The Japanese call it Gunkanjima, Battleship Island. At its peak in 1959, 5,259 people lived on it, making it the most densely populated place on earth. In 1974, the coal ran out. The last residents left in April. No one has lived there since.

Scene 1 — The Island from the Sea

A boat moves across gray water toward an island. From a distance the island looks industrial — a long horizontal form with towers rising from it, the whole thing encased in concrete. The camera holds from the bow of the approaching boat as the island grows larger. It does not look abandoned. It looks like a city that has been set afloat.

SETTING: The East China Sea, nine miles from Nagasaki — approach by boat
SUBJECT: Hashima Island seen from the water, growing larger
IN-SCENE MOTION: The bow of the boat rises and falls gently; the island fills more of the frame with each swell
LIGHTING: Overcast morning; the concrete catches no warmth; the sea is pewter-gray
NARRATION BEAT: From this distance, it looks occupied. The buildings are all still standing. The seawall is intact. Nothing about it announces that no one is home.

Scene 2 — The Shaft, 1930

A cage descends into a vertical shaft. Miners in cloth caps grip the bars. The walls of the shaft pass in the lamplight — wet rock, timber supports, the smell of coal and salt water. The camera rides down with the cage, the surface receding overhead.

SETTING: The coal shaft beneath Hashima Island, circa 1930
SUBJECT: Miners descending to the undersea seam
IN-SCENE MOTION: The cage drops; the walls move upward; the overhead light shrinks to a point
LIGHTING: A single shaft lamp from above; the sides of the cage and the miners' faces lit from that one source, everything else dark
NARRATION BEAT: The coal seam ran beneath the seafloor. Miners worked hundreds of meters below the ocean, cutting coal from rock that had the sea pressing down on it from above.

Scene 3 — The Apartment Block, 1959

A corridor in a high-rise apartment building, 1959. Narrow, fluorescent-lit, doors close together on both sides. Children run past. A woman carries a basket of laundry. The sound of a radio through one door. The camera tracks slowly down the corridor, the activity of the building moving around it.

SETTING: Apartment building 65, Hashima — the largest residential structure
SUBJECT: The corridor at peak population
IN-SCENE MOTION: Children pass; a door opens and closes; a woman moves past with laundry; the fluorescent light hums
LIGHTING: Institutional fluorescent strip lighting; the corridor windows at each end show a sliver of ocean
NARRATION BEAT: In 1959, the island had 5,259 residents — a density exceeding Manhattan's. There was a school, a hospital, a pachinko parlor, a cinema, eight restaurants, a barbershop. There was no room for anything to be far away.

Scene 4 — The Rooftop Garden

A rooftop on the island, 1960s. A small garden: potted plants, a child's bicycle, a folding chair. A woman waters a plant. Beyond the rooftop edge, the ocean in every direction. The camera holds in a static wide shot — the rooftop garden, the woman, the sea.

SETTING: A residential rooftop, Hashima Island
SUBJECT: A small garden tended on the roof — the only open space
IN-SCENE MOTION: A woman pours water from a tin can; the plants move slightly; the ocean is still
LIGHTING: Midday sun, direct; the rooftop is bright; the shadow of the water tower falls across one corner
NARRATION BEAT: There was no park. No open ground. Everything was concrete and corridor. Residents grew what they could on their rooftops — small gardens against the sky and the sea.

Scene 5 — April 1974

A dock. A ferry is loaded with furniture, boxes, household goods. Families carry things down a gangway. An older man looks back at the buildings above the dock. The ferry horn sounds. The camera holds on the gangway as the last people walk down it.

SETTING: The Hashima dock, April 1974 — the final departure
SUBJECT: The last residents leaving; the island behind them
IN-SCENE MOTION: People carry boxes; the gangway sways slightly; the ferry horn sounds once and the camera holds after the sound fades
LIGHTING: Morning light; the sun behind the buildings; the dock in shadow
NARRATION BEAT: Mitsubishi announced the closure in January 1974. By April, the island was empty. The process took weeks. People packed what they could. Everything else was left.

Scene 6 — The Corridor, Now

The same corridor from Scene 3, fifty years later. The fluorescent lights are gone — or rather, they are still there but the tubes have shattered. Light comes through a gap in the wall where concrete has fallen. The floor is covered in debris — broken glass, plaster, a child's shoe. The camera tracks slowly down the corridor exactly as before.

SETTING: The same corridor, Hashima Island, present day
SUBJECT: The same space, abandoned for fifty years
IN-SCENE MOTION: The camera tracks at the same pace as in Scene 3; dust moves in the shaft of daylight; nothing else moves
LIGHTING: A single shaft of daylight from a structural gap; deep shadow elsewhere; no artificial light
NARRATION BEAT: No one stripped the buildings. No one came back for what was left. They simply closed the door and the ferry sailed.

Scene 7 — The Calendar

A small apartment room. A calendar on the wall — the page shows April 1974. The number 20 is circled. Below the calendar, a low table with a teacup, a folded newspaper. The camera slowly pushes in on the calendar, the circled date filling the frame.

SETTING: An abandoned apartment, Hashima Island
SUBJECT: A calendar left on the wall; a date circled
IN-SCENE MOTION: The camera pushes in slowly; the circled date grows until it fills the frame; a piece of the plaster wall has fallen near the calendar but the calendar remains
LIGHTING: Diffuse gray light from a small window; the room is dim; the calendar is the brightest object
NARRATION BEAT: Someone circled the 20th. It is not known why.

Scene 8 — The Island Receding

The same boat, returning. The island grows smaller in the frame as the boat pulls away. The towers are still visible, still vertical. The seawall holds. The camera tracks from the stern as Hashima shrinks back into the sea.

SETTING: The East China Sea — departure
SUBJECT: Hashima Island receding behind the boat
IN-SCENE MOTION: The wake of the boat churns in the foreground; the island grows smaller; the camera holds at the stern
LIGHTING: Late afternoon; the concrete catches more gold than in the morning; the island is warmer now, receding
NARRATION BEAT: The island is still there. It is not going anywhere. It is simply waiting, which is different from being abandoned — though no one is sure what it is waiting for.

Key Takeaway
Hashima Island is a record of an entire way of life preserved at the moment of its end. The people left too quickly and in too much order to take everything with them, and so the island still contains everything it ever was — a functional city, completely intact, in which nothing works and no one lives. It is not a ruin in the usual sense. It is a city that has been paused.
