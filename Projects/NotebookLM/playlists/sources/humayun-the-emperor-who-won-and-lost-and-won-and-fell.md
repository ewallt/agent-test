# Humayun: The Emperor Who Won and Lost and Won and Fell

Humayun, second emperor of the Mughal dynasty, spent fifteen years as a wandering exile after losing his empire in a military disaster of his own making. He fought his way back across Persia and Afghanistan, reconquered everything he had lost, sat on the throne of Delhi again — and died four months later, falling down a staircase while carrying a pile of books. He was 47.

---

Scene 1 — The Empire He Inherited

1530. Humayun inherits the Mughal Empire from his father Babur — a domain stretching from Kabul to the Ganges plain. He is 22 years old, educated, cultured, a lover of astronomy and poetry, fluent in Persian and Turkic. He is also indecisive, prone to delay, and surrounded by brothers who want what he has. His father reportedly warned him on his deathbed to treat his brothers gently. SUBJECT: The young Humayun on the throne

LIGHTING: Filtered light through latticed screens; warm and diffuse
NARRATION BEAT: Everything his father built is now his. He is about to spend fifteen years losing it.

---

Scene 2 — Sher Shah and the Disaster at Chausa

1539. Humayun is facing Sher Shah Suri — an Afghan chieftain who has been consolidating power in Bengal and Bihar for years. At the Battle of Chausa, Humayun camps on the wrong side of the Ganges during the monsoon season. Sher Shah attacks at night. The Mughal army is caught in the rising river on one side and the enemy on the other. Humayun flees on horseback and nearly drowns crossing the Ganges — he is saved, according to one account, by a water-carrier who inflated his animal-skin water bag and used it to float the emperor across. The camera tracks through a night river crossing — dark water, torchlight, figures struggling in the current.

SETTING: The Ganges River, night of June 26, 1539
SUBJECT: Humayun fleeing across the Ganges after the defeat at Chausa
IN-SCENE MOTION: Men in dark water; a figure clinging to an inflated skin; the current pulling
LIGHTING: Near darkness; a single torch on the far bank; moonlight on the water
NARRATION BEAT: He survives. His army does not.

---

Scene 3 — Fifteen Years in the Wilderness

1540. Humayun loses a second decisive battle at Kanauj and flees west. What follows is fifteen years of exile — wandering through Sindh, Rajputana, Persia, Afghanistan. He is sheltered and then expelled. He begs allies for armies. He loses those armies. He gains a son — Akbar, born in a desert on the run, who will one day be the greatest Mughal emperor of all — and nearly loses him too. The camera tracks slowly alongside a small ragged group moving across a flat desert — dust, heat, minimal provisions, one figure slightly ahead of the rest, still wearing the faded remnants of imperial dress.

SETTING: The deserts of Sindh, 1540s
SUBJECT: Humayun in exile — a small, diminished party moving through harsh terrain
IN-SCENE MOTION: The group moving steadily; heat shimmer on the horizon; a flag hanging limp in the heat
LIGHTING: Brutal midday sun; flat shadowless light
NARRATION BEAT: He was given an empire. He lost it in two battles. He has been walking for two years.

---

Scene 4 — The Persian Alliance

1544. Humayun reaches the court of Shah Tahmasp of Persia — the one ruler powerful enough to give him what he needs. The Persians are Shia Muslim; the Mughals are Sunni. The price of Persian military support is Humayun converting, at least nominally, to Shia Islam for the duration of his stay. He agrees. He gets 12,000 Persian cavalry. The camera holds in the Persian court — tilework, fountains, formal ceremony — on the handshake between two rulers who need each other and trust each other barely at all.

SETTING: The Safavid court in Tabriz, Persia, 1544
SUBJECT: Humayun and Shah Tahmasp negotiating the alliance
IN-SCENE MOTION: Courtiers moving; a translator speaking; the two rulers at the center of the frame
LIGHTING: Interior court lighting — tile reflections, water light from a garden fountain
NARRATION BEAT: He is trading his religion for an army. He will trade it back when he no longer needs it.

---

Scene 5 — The Reconquest

1545 to 1555. With Persian support, Humayun retakes Kandahar and Kabul. He builds his forces. He waits. When Sher Shah Suri dies in 1545 — killed by an exploding gunpowder magazine at a siege — the Sur dynasty fragments. In 1555, Humayun marches back into India. The Sur claimants cannot unite against him. He defeats them in two engagements. In July 1555, he re-enters Delhi. The camera tracks alongside a mounted procession through the gates of a great city — crowds, banners, Humayun at the center, 47 years old and returned.

SETTING: Delhi, July 1555
SUBJECT: Humayun re-entering his capital after fifteen years in exile
IN-SCENE MOTION: The procession moving through crowded streets; banners overhead; people pressing to see
LIGHTING: Bright summer sun; dust and color; a city celebrating
NARRATION BEAT: He waited fifteen years to walk through this gate again. He has four months left.

---

Scene 6 — The Library

January 1556. It is an evening in Delhi. Humayun has been back on the throne for six months. He is in the library of his palace — the Sher Mandal, a small octagonal tower he has converted into a reading room and observatory. He is carrying an armful of books down the narrow stone staircase from the upper story when he hears the call to prayer. He moves to kneel. His foot catches on the hem of his robe. He falls. The camera pushes in very slowly on the staircase from below — the narrow stone steps, the worn edge of one step, the light from above.

SETTING: The Sher Mandal library tower, Delhi, January 27, 1556
SUBJECT: Humayun descending the stairs
IN-SCENE MOTION: A figure beginning to descend; the motion slowing; books beginning to fall
LIGHTING: Warm interior lamplight; the stairwell half in shadow
NARRATION BEAT: He hit his head on the stone. He died three days later.

---

Scene 7 — What He Left Behind

Humayun died at 47. His son Akbar was 13. Within fifty years, Akbar would build the greatest version of the Mughal Empire — an era of religious tolerance, architectural brilliance, and administrative sophistication that his father only glimpsed. Humayun had spent two-thirds of his reign in exile and the remaining third getting back to the starting point. He reconquered an empire, sat down on the throne, and fell down a staircase four months later. The camera pulls back slowly from the Sher Mandal tower at dawn — a small octagonal building in a large garden, the city beyond it, quiet.

SETTING: The Sher Mandal tower at dawn, Delhi
SUBJECT: The building where Humayun died — now empty and still
IN-SCENE MOTION: Morning birds moving; light slowly filling the garden; the tower unchanged
LIGHTING: Blue pre-dawn light warming to gold; long slow transition
NARRATION BEAT: He won the empire back. The staircase was four feet wide and twelve steps high.

---

Key Takeaway

Humayun spent fifteen years losing his empire and working to reclaim it. He succeeded completely — then died four months later falling down a staircase in his own library. He left behind a son named Akbar who would build the greatest Mughal Empire in history. The man who survived exile, betrayal, and two crushing military defeats was undone by a step.
