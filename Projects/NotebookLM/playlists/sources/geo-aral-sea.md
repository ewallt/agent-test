# The Aral Sea: How an Engineering Decision Drained the Fourth Largest Lake on Earth

In the 1960s, Soviet planners diverted the rivers that fed the Aral Sea to irrigate cotton fields across Central Asia. Within forty years, one of the world's largest lakes had mostly ceased to exist. What remained was salt flats, stranded ships, and a poisoned landscape — a monument to what happens when a resource is treated as unlimited and the people who depend on it have no voice in the plan.

Scene 1 — A Lake the Size of a Sea

A vast blue expanse fills the frame as the camera tilts up slowly from shoreline to open water, revealing a lake so large the far shore disappears in haze. This is the Aral Sea in 1960: the fourth largest lake on earth, 68,000 square kilometers, home to 24 species of fish and a fishing industry that employed tens of thousands across Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan. The port town of Moynaq sits at the southern shore. Fishing boats rock gently at the docks. Seagulls wheel overhead in bright midday sun.

SUBJECT: Fishing boats docked at a thriving port on the southern Aral Sea
IN-SCENE MOTION: Boats rocking on the water; seagulls wheeling in arcs above the docks

NARRATION BEAT: Establishing what existed before the decision — the scale and vitality of the lake

Scene 2 — The Cotton Decision

In Moscow in the early 1960s, Soviet agricultural planners study a map of Central Asia. They are staring at two rivers: the Amu Darya and the Syr Darya, both flowing into the Aral Sea. The plan is to divert them — to build thousands of kilometers of irrigation canals across the steppe and grow cotton at industrial scale. The Aral Sea is not a concern. It is simply water that can be put to better use. A hand draws a line on the map. The camera holds on it as the line extends across the page.

SETTING: Soviet government planning office, Moscow, early 1960s
SUBJECT: A planner's hand tracing an irrigation route on a large regional map
IN-SCENE MOTION: A hand drawing slowly across the map; papers rustling in a background draft
LIGHTING: Cool fluorescent office light, one desk lamp casting a warm pool on the map
NARRATION BEAT: The decision that will drain the lake — ideological, economic, irreversible

Scene 3 — The Rivers Are Turned

Across the steppe, earthmoving equipment carves canals wider than highways. The Amu Darya, which once ran to the Aral Sea, is redirected — its water fanning out across cotton fields in Uzbekistan and Turkmenistan. Soviet production reports glow with success. Cotton yields climb. What the reports do not show is what is happening to the lake, hundreds of kilometers away, as inflow drops and the sun continues to evaporate the surface. The water level falls one meter in the first year. Then another. Then another. The camera holds on the excavation from a distance as dust rises from the machinery.

SETTING: Central Asian steppe, 1960s — irrigation canal construction
SUBJECT: Heavy earthmoving machinery cutting a massive canal through flat semi-arid terrain
IN-SCENE MOTION: Machinery moving earth; dust rising in columns; workers moving alongside
LIGHTING: Harsh midday desert light; haze and dust softening the horizon
NARRATION BEAT: The scale of the engineering project — and what is quietly beginning at the lake

Scene 4 — The Shoreline Retreats

By the early 1980s, the fishing boats at Moynaq cannot reach the water. The shoreline has retreated — first by a few kilometers, then more. Fishermen walk out across flat, cracked earth where their boats once floated. The fish processing plants continue operating for a time, importing fish from other seas. Then they close. A tracking shot follows a fisherman walking across the exposed seabed, his boots raising small clouds of white dust with each step. He stops. In all directions there is nothing but flat salt and sky. He does not turn back. He just stands there.

SETTING: Former shoreline at Moynaq, Uzbekistan, early 1980s
SUBJECT: A fisherman walking across the dry seabed where the lake once was
IN-SCENE MOTION: Slow walking; white dust rising underfoot; cracked salt crust fracturing with each step
LIGHTING: Flat overcast sky; no shadows; a pale, colorless horizon
NARRATION BEAT: The human experience of watching a lake disappear

Scene 5 — The Ship Graveyard

Near Moynaq, a fleet of fishing trawlers sits in the middle of the desert. Their hulls are rusted, their paint peeling in long strips. Some have collapsed onto their sides. The camera moves through them at ground level — these ships are enormous up close, disorienting in a landscape with no water anywhere. Some still bear their Soviet-era names in faded stencil. They never moved. The water simply left them behind. Wind moves through the loose metal panels, producing a low moan. Sand drifts against the hulls in low ridges. They have become the most photographed symbol of environmental catastrophe of the twentieth century.

SETTING: Ship graveyard near Moynaq, Uzbekistan — abandoned vessels in the desert
SUBJECT: Rusted Soviet fishing trawlers stranded in the sand
IN-SCENE MOTION: Wind flexing loose metal panels; sand shifting along the base of the hulls
LIGHTING: Low-angle late afternoon light casting long shadows from the hulls across the sand
NARRATION BEAT: The visual emblem of what happened — ships in a desert

Scene 6 — Salt Storms

As the lake shrank, its exposed bed became a source of danger. The Aral Sea had concentrated salts and pesticide residue from decades of agricultural runoff. Now those compounds sit dry and loose on the surface. When the wind comes — and across Central Asia, the wind always comes — it picks up the salt and carries it across the region in vast grey plumes. Children in surrounding villages develop chronic lung disease. Infant mortality climbs. The salt reaches distant farmland and ruins it. The camera holds on a wide aerial view of a salt storm rolling outward from the exposed seabed, the front edge turning ochre in the low sun.

SETTING: Central Asian steppe — aerial view of a salt and dust storm rolling off the exposed seabed
SUBJECT: A massive dust plume rising from the white salt flats
IN-SCENE MOTION: The storm front rolling outward across flat terrain; the leading edge billowing upward
LIGHTING: Low sun turning the dust plume amber and ochre at the edges; dark sky behind
NARRATION BEAT: The second-order catastrophe — health, agriculture, the cascading effects beyond the lake

Scene 7 — Kazakhstan Builds a Dam

In 2005, Kazakhstan completed the Kokaral Dam — a dike built across the narrow strait separating the northern and southern portions of what remained of the Aral Sea. The North Aral Sea, still fed by the Syr Darya, began to fill. Within a decade the water level had risen twelve meters. Fish returned. A small fishing industry restarted. The camera pulls back slowly from the dam to show the recovered blue water behind it — and then continues pulling back to reveal the vast white salt flats of the southern basin, still empty, still dead. One part saved. The rest gone. The contrast is total.

SETTING: Kokaral Dam, Kazakhstan — the dam structure and the water behind it, aerial view
SUBJECT: The dam with the recovering North Aral Sea behind it and the barren southern basin in the distance
IN-SCENE MOTION: Water flowing through controlled dam outlets; distant ripples on the northern lake surface
LIGHTING: Clear blue sky; bright reflections off the northern water; white salt flats dull and matte beyond
NARRATION BEAT: The partial recovery — and what it reveals about what cannot be recovered

Scene 8 — The Accounting

A researcher in a small office lays out a series of satellite images: the Aral Sea in 1960, 1975, 1990, 2000, 2010, 2020. The camera pushes in slowly on the sequence. Each image shows less blue. The final frame is almost entirely white: salt flat, exposed seabed, the ghost of a lake. She sets down the last image and looks up. Outside her window, somewhere far to the east, there is still water. But the sea her grandparents knew — the one with the fishing boats and the seagulls — is gone.

SETTING: Research office, contemporary — a desk covered in printed satellite images
SUBJECT: A researcher reviewing the shrinkage sequence, image by image
IN-SCENE MOTION: Hands moving images across the desk; slow camera push toward the final image

---

Key Takeaway

The Aral Sea was not drained by accident — it was drained by a decision. Soviet planners chose cotton over the lake, and within forty years the fourth largest body of freshwater on earth had mostly ceased to exist. The ship graveyard near Moynaq stands as a monument to what happens when a resource is treated as unlimited — and when the people who depend on it have no voice in the plan.
