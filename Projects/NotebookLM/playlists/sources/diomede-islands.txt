The Diomede Islands — Two Miles, Two Days

Three miles of open water separate Big Diomede from Little Diomede in the Bering Strait. The two islands are close enough to see clearly on most days — close enough, on a calm winter, to walk between them on the ice. But they sit on opposite sides of the International Date Line. When it is Tuesday afternoon on Little Diomede, it is Wednesday afternoon on Big Diomede. The same gray water, the same cold, two different days. And since 1948, no one has made the crossing.

Scene 1 — The Strait

The Bering Strait, late summer. The water is steel-gray and choppy under a low sky. Two dark shapes rise from the water — one to the west, one to the east — less than three miles apart. Both are steep-sided, rocky, crowned with cloud. The camera tracks slowly from one island to the other in a single long pan, holding the gap between them.

SETTING: The Bering Strait, between Alaska and Siberia
SUBJECT: Both islands visible simultaneously from the water
IN-SCENE MOTION: Low waves move through the frame; the camera tracks west to east across the strait
LIGHTING: Overcast daylight, flat and cold; the water absorbs the light rather than reflecting it
NARRATION BEAT: Three miles. On a clear day you can see one from the other. You are also looking at two different tomorrows.

Scene 2 — Discovery, 1728

A wooden ship pitches in a heavy swell. The year is 1728. Vitus Bering, a Danish captain sailing under the Russian flag, is attempting to determine whether Asia and America are connected by land. On August 16, he passes between two islands. He names the larger one for the feast day of Saint Diomede. He does not see the American coast through the fog. He turns around. The camera holds on the stern of the ship as it pushes west, the islands receding into the mist.

SETTING: The Bering Strait, August 1728, aboard a Russian exploration vessel
SUBJECT: The stern of the ship; the two islands disappearing behind it
IN-SCENE MOTION: The wake of the ship churns in the foreground; the mist closes in at the edges of the frame
LIGHTING: Dull silver daylight filtering through sea fog
NARRATION BEAT: He found the strait. He did not find the answer. It would take another expedition, fifteen years later, to reach Alaska.

Scene 3 — Little Diomede

The American island. A cluster of wooden houses built directly against a steep rock face, above the narrow beach — the only flat ground on the island. Children in bright jackets move along a path between the houses. A dog pulls against its tether. The camera tracks slowly across the village front, the Bering Strait visible and close behind.

SETTING: Little Diomede, Alaska — the Inupiaq village of Diomede
SUBJECT: The village against the cliff, daily life
IN-SCENE MOTION: A child walks past; a flag moves in the wind at the edge of the frame
LIGHTING: Pale Arctic daylight, the rock face in shadow, the houses catching the available light
NARRATION BEAT: About 135 people live here. They have lived here for at least 3,000 years. The Inupiaq name for the island is Ignaluk — the one over there.

Scene 4 — Big Diomede, Three Miles West

The Russian island, seen from the water. No houses. No lights. A military weather station near the summit, barely visible. The shoreline is steep and uninhabited. The camera holds in a slow push-in toward the island, which grows but does not clarify.

SETTING: Big Diomede (Ratmanov Island), Russia — seen from across the strait
SUBJECT: The empty Russian island, no civilian habitation
IN-SCENE MOTION: The camera pushes in slowly; the dark rock grows larger without becoming more legible
LIGHTING: Flat gray light; the island sits in its own shadow
NARRATION BEAT: The Soviet Union expelled the Yupik people of Big Diomede in 1948. No civilians have lived there since. It is a military installation.

Scene 5 — The Ice Curtain

February. The strait has frozen — a jagged plain of pressure ice connecting the two islands. The camera looks out across the ice from the American shore. In the distance, the outline of Big Diomede is just visible. A single set of boot tracks leads toward it, then stops. The tracks do not continue.

SETTING: The frozen Bering Strait, winter
SUBJECT: The ice connecting the islands; footprints that stop
IN-SCENE MOTION: Wind pushes a thin scrim of loose snow across the surface of the ice
LIGHTING: White winter light, low and directionless; the far island is a gray shape
NARRATION BEAT: For centuries, Inupiaq and Yupik people crossed freely between the islands — trading, intermarrying, visiting family. The Soviet closure in 1948 ended it overnight. Families on opposite sides of the strait were permanently separated.

Scene 6 — The Date Line

A map on a table, hand-drawn, showing both islands and the International Date Line running between them. A finger traces the line — it bends slightly eastward between the islands so that both fall into their respective nations' time zones. The camera slowly pulls back from the map until the whole strait is visible.

SETTING: A map, close-up
SUBJECT: The International Date Line between the two islands
IN-SCENE MOTION: A finger traces the date line slowly from north to south; the camera pulls back
LIGHTING: Even reading-room light on the paper
NARRATION BEAT: The date line was adjusted here deliberately. Stand on Little Diomede and look west. You are not looking at yesterday or tomorrow in any poetic sense. You are looking at a different calendar day. Right now.

Scene 7 — The Crossing That Doesn't Happen

A small boat sits pulled up on the beach at Little Diomede. It is pointed toward the water but not launched. An older man stands beside it, looking west. He does not move. The camera holds in a static medium shot, the man and the boat and the strait all in frame.

SETTING: The shore of Little Diomede, present day
SUBJECT: A boat not in the water; a man looking west
IN-SCENE MOTION: The water moves; the man is still; the boat is still
LIGHTING: Afternoon light from the south; long shadows on the gravel
NARRATION BEAT: The crossing is three miles. In good conditions, it takes under an hour. There is no legal way to make it. The American side requires a US passport. The Russian side requires a Russian visa, applied for months in advance, rarely granted. The people who once crossed freely cannot cross at all.

Scene 8 — Two Clocks

Two analog clocks on a wall, side by side. One reads 3:00 p.m. The other reads 3:00 p.m. — but beneath it, a small label reads a different day. The camera holds on both clock faces in a steady medium shot, neither moving.

SETTING: A bare interior — two clocks on a wall
SUBJECT: Identical times, different days
IN-SCENE MOTION: Both second hands sweep — one goes clockwise, one goes clockwise — both telling the same time, different days
LIGHTING: Flat interior light; the clocks are the only thing in the frame
NARRATION BEAT: The strangeness of the Diomedes is not that the crossing is forbidden, or that the islands are so close. It is that three miles of cold water is enough to put you in two completely different days at once — and no one can agree whether that is a curiosity or a tragedy.

Key Takeaway
The Diomede Islands are the clearest physical demonstration of the arbitrary nature of the systems humans use to organize time and territory. The International Date Line and the Cold War border run through the same three miles of water, turning a short crossing into an international incident and a single community into a permanent family separation. The islands are still there. The ice still forms. The people on Little Diomede can still see the Russian island clearly. They just cannot go.
