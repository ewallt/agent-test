# Drive: Why Carrots and Sticks Fail

Daniel Pink's *Drive* dismantles one of the most persistent assumptions in management and education: that rewards and punishments reliably motivate people. The science has known for fifty years that this is false for complex, creative work — and yet almost every organization on earth is still running on the old model. The video traces the evidence from the laboratory to the office floor, and asks why the gap between what we know and what we do is so wide.

---

Scene 1 — The Candle on the Wall

A researcher places three objects on a table: a candle, a box of thumbtacks, and a book of matches. The task is to attach the candle to the wall so that it burns without dripping on the table below. Most people try to tack the candle directly to the wall, or melt the wax and stick it there. Neither works. The solution is to empty the box, tack it to the wall, and use it as a platform. When subjects are told they will be paid if they solve it quickly, they take longer. The financial incentive narrows thinking — and the box, which is also a container, becomes invisible.

A plain table under fluorescent light. A candle, a matchbox, and a handful of thumbtacks arranged in a neat row. A figure sits, studying them. The camera holds steady on the table as a hand reaches forward, hesitates, and withdraws.

SETTING: A bare psychology lab, 1962, fluorescent overhead light
SUBJECT: A test subject staring at three objects on a table
IN-SCENE MOTION: The subject's hand reaching toward the box, pausing, pulling back
LIGHTING: Flat institutional white, no shadows
NARRATION BEAT: The candle problem — the incentive narrows thinking, and the solution disappears

Scene 2 — The Monkeys and the Puzzle

In 1949, the primatologist Harry Harlow places a series of simple mechanical puzzles in a group of rhesus monkey cages. He expects nothing to happen — there is no food reward, no punishment, no training. Within hours, the monkeys are solving the puzzles. They are doing it correctly. They are doing it repeatedly. And they appear to be enjoying it. Harlow calls the phenomenon intrinsic motivation — a third drive, beyond hunger and beyond fear. The scientific establishment disagrees, sets the finding aside, and moves on. The finding waits thirty years to be reopened.

A single puzzle box sits on a shelf inside a cage under soft light. A macaque approaches it, turns it over in its hands, and begins working the latch. No handler is present. No food is offered. The camera holds in a shallow rack focus — the puzzle sharp in frame, the monkey's face slightly soft behind it.

SETTING: A primate research facility, 1949, natural cage light
SUBJECT: A rhesus monkey working a mechanical puzzle alone
IN-SCENE MOTION: The monkey's hands turning and pressing the latch, ears forward, eyes intent
LIGHTING: Warm ambient cage light, slightly golden
NARRATION BEAT: Harlow's discovery — a drive that required no reward, and that the field chose to ignore

---

Scene 3 — The Payment That Backfired

Edward Deci runs a two-week experiment at the University of Rochester in the early 1970s. He gives college students a set of Soma puzzle cubes. One group is paid for each puzzle they solve. The other group is not. When the payment stops, the paid group loses interest almost immediately. The unpaid group keeps going. The reward had not created motivation — it had destroyed the motivation that was already there. Deci calls it the overjustification effect: when external incentives are introduced into work someone is already doing for internal reasons, the internal reasons evacuate.

A student sits alone in a room with a set of colored geometric blocks arranged on a table, some assembled, some scattered. He is absorbed, oblivious to the camera. Then a note slides under the door. He reads it, looks back at the blocks, and slowly pushes his chair back. The camera holds on the blocks, undisturbed, as the student's hands retreat from the frame.

SETTING: A university study room, 1970s, afternoon light through a frosted window
SUBJECT: A student working with Soma puzzle cubes
IN-SCENE MOTION: The student reading a note, withdrawing; the blocks remaining on the table, untouched
LIGHTING: Warm afternoon glow through glass, slightly diffuse
NARRATION BEAT: The overjustification effect — reward undermines the motivation it was meant to create

Scene 4 — What Rewards Are Good For

An assembly line worker repeats the same motion every twelve seconds. Reach, position, fasten, release. The task is unambiguous, the correct answer is defined, and the output is measurable. For this kind of work — Pink calls it algorithmic — extrinsic rewards function exactly as advertised. They increase output. They focus attention. They get results. The science is not against incentives for simple, repetitive tasks. It is against using the same tool for work that requires creativity, judgment, and problem-solving — heuristic work, where the right answer is not known in advance.

A factory floor in operation. One worker repeats the same hand motion with practiced efficiency — reach, position, fasten, release. The camera tracks smoothly alongside the line, keeping the worker's hands in frame, the rhythm precise and unbroken.

SETTING: A manufacturing plant, mid-century, warm industrial light
SUBJECT: A single worker at an assembly station, hands in repetitive motion
IN-SCENE MOTION: The worker's consistent, fluid cycle; components moving down the line
LIGHTING: Warm tungsten factory light from overhead, steady and even
NARRATION BEAT: Where rewards work — algorithmic tasks with clear right answers and measurable outputs

Scene 5 — Autonomy

In 2006, the software company Atlassian tries an experiment. Engineers are given twenty-four hours — one full day — with no assignments, no deliverables, and no agenda. They can work on anything they want. At the end of the day, they present what they built. The session produces more genuinely useful product improvements than any equivalent span of structured development time. FedEx Days, as Atlassian calls them, become a permanent fixture. The engineers had not been given more resources. They had been given control of their time — and the work that emerged was better for it.

A row of desks late in the evening. Most are empty. One engineer sits at hers, surrounded by coffee cups and handwritten notes, intent on something on her screen. The camera slowly pushes in past the empty desks toward her, the rest of the office falling soft and dark behind.

SUBJECT: A lone software engineer working voluntarily, absorbed
IN-SCENE MOTION: Her fingers on the keyboard; the faint glow of the screen on her face
LIGHTING: Single desk lamp warm against dark surroundings; screen glow
NARRATION BEAT: Autonomy — the first element; control over time, task, technique, and team produces better work

Scene 6 — Mastery

Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi spent decades studying what people describe as their best moments of experience. Not relaxation — engagement. The state they call "flow" occurs when a challenge and a person's skill are precisely balanced: too easy, and attention drifts; too hard, and anxiety takes over. The narrow band in between is where time disappears. Pink calls mastery asymptotic — a line that approaches but never reaches a ceiling. No one becomes a perfect musician, or a perfect surgeon, or a perfect teacher. But the progress itself is the reward. This is why people practice for decades in disciplines they will never be paid for.

A musician sits alone in a practice room, playing the same four bars of a difficult passage repeatedly. Not performing — working. On the third attempt, something changes in his posture: a slight release in the shoulders, a shift in the wrist. The camera holds in a slow push-in from the doorway as the phrase finally resolves cleanly, once, then again.

SETTING: A practice room, late afternoon, natural light through a single high window
SUBJECT: A musician alone, rehearsing a difficult passage at a piano or cello
IN-SCENE MOTION: The physical shift — shoulders dropping, a phrase finally landing cleanly
LIGHTING: Warm late-afternoon light raking through the high window, falling across hands
NARRATION BEAT: Mastery — the asymptotic drive; the getting-better is itself the reward

Scene 7 — Purpose

The researchers who design the most effective motivation studies share something with the people who report the highest job satisfaction: they can articulate why the work matters beyond the work itself. Pink calls it purpose — the third element of intrinsic motivation. Organizations that build around a genuine "why" — not as marketing language, but as the actual reason the institution exists — outperform those that optimize purely for profit. This is not idealism. It is what the science predicts for the kind of work the modern economy runs on.

A hospital corridor at night. A cleaner moves slowly along the floor with a mop, unhurried. A researcher who has studied hospital cleaners extensively explains what distinguishes those who stay in the job from those who leave: not the wage, but whether they understand the role as part of the care. The camera tilts up slowly from the moving mop to the cleaner's face — steady, intent, at home.

SUBJECT: A hospital cleaner, working alone, unhurried
IN-SCENE MOTION: The mop moving in slow, deliberate arcs; the cleaner glancing toward a patient room door
LIGHTING: Clinical fluorescent overhead, softened at the edges
NARRATION BEAT: Purpose — the connection to something beyond the task; what separates people who stay from those who leave

Scene 8 — What Science Knows

Pink's central argument is not a new discovery. The experiments go back to the 1940s. Deci's work was published in 1971. The gap between what motivational science established and what management practice does with it is not a gap of knowledge. Every major business school has taught this research for decades. Every human resources department has access to it. And yet the bonus structures, the performance incentives, the if-then reward systems remain exactly as they were when Frederick Winslow Taylor designed them for a factory floor in 1911. The mismatch is not about ignorance. It is about inertia.

A large open-plan office, full of workers at their desks. The camera pulls back slowly through a glass wall, widening to show the whole floor — row after row of identical stations, monitored, measured, and incentivized according to principles designed for an assembly line. The figures inside, lit by their screens, do not look up.

SETTING: A modern corporate office, full floor, glass-walled
SUBJECT: Dozens of knowledge workers at their stations
IN-SCENE MOTION: The slow pull-back; figures moving in peripheral vision; the scale of the floor emerging
LIGHTING: Screen glow across the whole floor; overhead fluorescent from above; the exterior through glass, dark
NARRATION BEAT: The gap — we know the science, and we run on the old model anyway

---

Key Takeaway

The science of motivation is not complicated: autonomy, mastery, and purpose produce better work than rewards and punishments for any task that requires genuine thinking. *Drive* is an argument that the most consequential mismatch in the modern economy is not a resource problem — it is a management philosophy that was designed for a world that no longer exists.
