# The Forgotten Petri Dish: How a Vacation and an Untidy Lab Changed Medicine

In the summer of 1928, Alexander Fleming left his laboratory at St. Mary's Hospital in London for a two-week vacation without cleaning his bench. He stacked used petri dishes in the sink — a habit his colleagues found irritating — and left. When he returned in September, one of those dishes had changed the history of medicine.

## The Man and the Mess

Fleming was a bacteriologist — competent, methodical, and known to be untidy. He had spent years studying Staphylococcus aureus, the bacterium responsible for blood poisoning, boils, and infected wounds. In 1928 he was culturing it on a series of petri dishes, part of ongoing research.

His lab on the second floor of St. Mary's was not a hermetically sealed environment. The lab directly below his was studying mold samples. Spores routinely drifted upward through the building.

Before Fleming left for vacation, he stacked his petri dishes rather than destroying them — standard practice was to autoclave used dishes immediately. He didn't. He went on holiday.

## The Discovery

When Fleming returned in early September, he began sorting through the stacked dishes. Most were contaminated — that was expected, given how long they'd been sitting. He was about to discard them when one dish caught his attention.

A blue-green mold had grown on it — later identified as Penicillium notatum. That alone was unremarkable. What was remarkable was the zone around the mold: the Staphylococcus colonies that had been growing on the plate were gone. Not crowded out — dissolved. The bacteria nearest the mold had been lysed: their cell walls had broken down. Something the mold was producing was killing them.

Fleming called a colleague over to look. He reportedly said: "That's funny."

He did not immediately grasp the full significance. What he recognized was an anomaly worth investigating. He kept the dish, cultured the mold, and began experiments to determine what it was producing and whether the substance — he called it penicillin — could kill other bacteria.

## The Limits of 1928

Fleming's subsequent research was promising but ran into a hard wall: he could not stabilize the active compound. Penicillin in its raw, "mold juice" form was too unstable and too impure to be used medically. It degraded quickly. It was difficult to produce in quantity. Fleming published his findings in 1929, noted the potential significance, and largely set the work aside.

For ten years, penicillin was a scientific curiosity — a published but dormant discovery. Fleming moved on to other research. The medical establishment largely ignored it. Some historians argue that if Fleming had been a chemist rather than a bacteriologist, he might have solved the purification problem himself. He wasn't, and he didn't.

## Florey and Chain

In 1938, Howard Florey, an Australian pathologist at Oxford, assigned his colleague Ernst Chain — a German-Jewish biochemist who had fled Nazi Germany — to survey the literature on naturally occurring antibacterial substances. Chain found Fleming's 1929 paper.

The timing was not coincidental. War was coming, and infected wounds killed soldiers. The incentive to find a treatment that could prevent sepsis was suddenly urgent in a way it hadn't been in peacetime.

By 1940, Florey and Chain had purified penicillin enough to test it. The first controlled experiment was conducted on eight mice, all infected with lethal doses of Streptococcus. Four received penicillin; four did not. The four untreated mice died. The four treated mice survived.

Florey reportedly said: "It looks like a miracle."

## The War and Mass Production

The first human trial was in February 1941: a police constable named Albert Alexander, dying of a Staphylococcal infection from a rose thorn scratch. He improved dramatically within days. The penicillin ran out. He relapsed and died.

But the proof was undeniable. The challenge was scale. Britain, under German bombardment, could not produce enough. Florey traveled to the United States in 1941 to enlist American pharmaceutical companies. Pfizer, Merck, Squibb, and others were recruited into a crash production program.

By D-Day in June 1944, the Allied forces had enough penicillin to treat every major infection among the invasion troops. Deaths from infected wounds — which had killed more soldiers than combat in every previous war — dropped to near zero.

## The Scale of What That Dish Produced

Penicillin and the antibiotics it made possible are estimated to have saved over 200 million lives since 1928. The figure is inherently imprecise, but the direction is not: before antibiotics, infection was the leading cause of death from injury, surgery, and childbirth. After antibiotics, it became manageable.

Scarlet fever, which killed tens of thousands annually in developed countries, became treatable. Syphilis, pneumonia, meningitis, tuberculosis — each was transformed from near-certain death to survivable condition.

## What the Dish Reveals

The story is often told as a triumph of serendipity — the right man in the right messy lab at the right moment. But the full story is more complicated and more instructive.

Fleming made the observation but could not complete the work. Florey and Chain completed the work but only because they found Fleming's dormant paper. The pharmaceutical companies scaled it only because a war created urgency. The production chain that saved 200 million lives required all three acts to happen in sequence.

What the forgotten petri dish reveals is not just that accidents can be discoveries. It is that a discovery without the infrastructure to develop it remains an accident. Fleming's mold sat in a journal for ten years because the mechanism for translating observation into medicine didn't yet exist — or wasn't yet motivated.

The dish was forgotten. The paper almost was too. The difference between those two outcomes — between a curiosity and a revolution — was a biochemist reading old literature in the shadow of an oncoming war.

## Key Facts

- Date of discovery: September 1928
- Location: St. Mary's Hospital, London, laboratory of Alexander Fleming
- Mold species: Penicillium notatum
- Original publication: Fleming, "On the Antibacterial Action of Cultures of a Penicillium," 1929
- Purification achieved by: Howard Florey and Ernst Chain, Oxford, 1940
- First human trial: February 1941 (Albert Alexander)
- Mass production: U.S. pharmaceutical companies, 1943–1944
- Nobel Prize: Fleming, Florey, and Chain, 1945
- Estimated lives saved: 200 million+
