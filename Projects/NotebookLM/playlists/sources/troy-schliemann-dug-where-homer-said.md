# Troy: He Dug Where Homer Said

For most of the nineteenth century, the scholarly consensus was clear: Troy was a myth. Homer's Iliad was a poem, not a history, and the city it described had never existed. In 1868, a self-made German businessman named Heinrich Schliemann went to western Turkey, opened Homer like a map, and started digging. He found Troy. He also, almost certainly, dug straight through it — destroying the actual Homeric city in his rush to reach it. This is the story of the man who was right for the wrong reasons, and what happens when an obsessive amateur succeeds where the professionals had given up.

---

Scene 1 — The Boy and the Picture

A modest German house, 1829. A seven-year-old boy sits at a kitchen table, a book open in front of him. It is a Christmas gift — a world history illustrated with engravings. He turns to a drawing of Troy in flames, walls collapsing, figures fleeing. He stares at it for a long time. His father leans over his shoulder and explains that Troy is fiction. The boy does not look up. The camera slowly pushes in on the child's face — serious, unconvinced — and holds there.

SETTING: Simple domestic interior, northern Germany, winter, candlelight
SUBJECT: Young Heinrich Schliemann, a book open before him
IN-SCENE MOTION: Pages turning, firelight flickering, father leaning in
LIGHTING: Warm candlelight, winter darkness outside the window
NARRATION BEAT: Heinrich Schliemann would later write that in that moment he decided Troy was real and that he would find it. Whether this is true or a story he told himself later, it became the organizing fact of his life.

Scene 2 — The Making of a Fortune

A montage of commerce — St. Petersburg, 1850s. A warehouse district at dawn, horses pulling carts, men unloading cargo. Schliemann moves through it all quickly — young, sharp-eyed, purposeful. He is buying and selling indigo, then saltpeter, then war contracts during the Crimean War. He is in California during the Gold Rush. He is in America during the Civil War. The camera tracks alongside him at street level, the world blurring past, as he moves through one city after another.

SETTING: St. Petersburg streets, warehouses, ports, 1850s
SUBJECT: Schliemann in motion — commerce, negotiation, movement
IN-SCENE MOTION: Street crowds, cargo being moved, Schliemann walking fast through it all
LIGHTING: Cold morning light in northern European cities
NARRATION BEAT: By his late forties, Schliemann had made and lost and remade several fortunes. He had taught himself eleven languages. And he had never stopped reading Homer. He retired from business in 1863 with enough money to spend the rest of his life looking for Troy.

Scene 3 — What the Scholars Said

A lecture hall, London or Berlin, 1860s. A scholar stands at a podium, gesturing at a map of the ancient world. He is explaining, patiently and with evident authority, that the Trojan War is mythology — a literary invention, not a historical event. The audience nods. The camera pans slowly along the rows of well-dressed academics, then cuts to a single empty seat near the back. Schliemann is not there.

SETTING: Lecture hall, European university, 1860s
SUBJECT: Scholar at podium, audience of academics
IN-SCENE MOTION: Lecturer gesturing, audience nodding, slow pan across faces
LIGHTING: Gaslight, formal and institutional
NARRATION BEAT: The academic consensus was not ignorant. There were real scholarly reasons to doubt. Homer wrote eight centuries after the supposed events. The Iliad was full of anachronisms. No physical evidence had ever been found. The consensus was wrong — but it was a reasonable wrong.

---

Scene 4 — The Hill at Hisarlik

Western Turkey, 1868. A low, unremarkable hill rising from a flat plain near the Turkish coast. Scrubby vegetation, wind, the distant shimmer of the Dardanelles. Schliemann stands on the summit with a copy of the Iliad open in his hand. He looks out at the plain below, then down at the page, then back at the plain. He is matching Homer's description of sight lines — where the Greeks beached their ships, where the battle took place, how far a runner could see from the walls. The camera slowly pulls back to show how flat the surrounding plain is, and how clearly visible everything would be from this one hill.

SETTING: Hisarlik hill, Troad region, Turkey, 1868, midday
SUBJECT: Schliemann on the hilltop with the Iliad
IN-SCENE MOTION: Wind moving through scrub grass, pages fluttering, Schliemann turning slowly
LIGHTING: Bright Turkish summer sun, hard shadows
NARRATION BEAT: Schliemann had identified Hisarlik as the likely site of Troy based purely on reading Homer. A British diplomat named Frank Calvert had independently reached the same conclusion and already owned part of the hill. Calvert had applied for excavation rights and been refused. He told Schliemann what he knew and helped him get permission to dig.

---

Scene 5 — The Digging

A massive excavation, 1872. Hundreds of Turkish workers with picks and shovels cutting a huge trench straight down through the hill — a trench thirty meters wide and ten meters deep. Dirt and rubble flying, wheelbarrows running on wooden rails. Schliemann stands at the edge of the trench, looking down, impatient. The camera tilts down from his face into the trench below — layer after layer of collapsed walls, potsherds, ash — the accumulated ruins of nine distinct cities stacked on top of each other.

SETTING: Hisarlik excavation site, 1872, full operation
SUBJECT: The trench — deep, wide, brutal
IN-SCENE MOTION: Hundreds of workers, dirt moving, wheelbarrows running
LIGHTING: Harsh Turkish sunlight, deep shadow in the trench
NARRATION BEAT: There were nine cities at Hisarlik, one on top of another — a stratigraphy of three thousand years. Schliemann was looking for the most dramatic one. He cut through most of them to reach it.

---

Scene 6 — Priam's Treasure

A dim excavation chamber, May 1873. Schliemann and his young Greek wife Sophia crouch over a discovery at the base of a wall — a cache of gold objects: diadems, goblets, earrings, bracelets. He begins to hand them to Sophia, who holds out her shawl to receive them. Gold in the candlelight. The camera pushes in slowly on the objects in the shawl — ancient gold glowing against rough cloth.

SETTING: Excavation shaft, below ground level, candlelight
SUBJECT: The gold objects being handed to Sophia
IN-SCENE MOTION: Hands passing objects, gold catching candlelight, shawl filling slowly
LIGHTING: Single candle in a confined earthen space, gold reflecting warmly
NARRATION BEAT: Schliemann called it Priam's Treasure and announced it to the world. He had his Troy. He was wrong about almost everything else. The gold was from a city 1,000 years too early to be Homer's Troy. The actual Homeric-era city — Troy VI or Troy VIIa — was the layer he had cut through and discarded to reach it.

---

Scene 7 — What He Got Right

The plain of Troy today. Late afternoon light raking across the excavated ruins — low stone walls, exposed foundations, the long view toward the water. A few tourists move slowly through the site. The camera slowly tilts up from the ruins to the plain beyond — the coastline visible in the distance, the route the Greek ships would have taken. The geography is exactly as Homer described it.

SETTING: Troy archaeological site, modern day, late afternoon
SUBJECT: The ruins and the plain beyond
IN-SCENE MOTION: Tourists moving, long grass in the wind, light shifting
LIGHTING: Golden hour, low angle, raking across stone
NARRATION BEAT: Schliemann was right that Troy was real, right about where it was, right that the hill held the ruins of a great Bronze Age city. He was careless with evidence, cavalier with stratigraphy, and probably guilty of smuggling the gold out of Turkey. Later excavations confirmed what he had disrupted. Troy was there. It had been there all along.

---

Key Takeaway

Heinrich Schliemann found Troy by treating Homer as a surveyor's manual rather than a poem — a method that no credentialed scholar would have attempted. He was right about the location and wrong about nearly everything else. The city had been visible in the ground for centuries. It took someone with nothing to lose, and a childhood certainty he never abandoned, to go and dig.
