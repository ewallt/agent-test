# Written in 1865, Launched in 1969: Jules Verne's Moon Rocket

In 1865, Jules Verne published a novel in which three men were launched to the moon from a gun embedded in the Florida peninsula. The capsule was made of aluminum, conical in shape, carried three crew members, and splashed down in the Pacific Ocean for recovery by the United States Navy. One hundred and four years later, NASA launched Apollo 8 from Cape Canaveral, Florida — three astronauts in an aluminum alloy capsule — and recovered it from the Pacific. The Apollo 11 command module was named Columbia. Verne's cannon was named the Columbiad. None of this was planned.

Scene 2 — The Columbiad

The novel's fictional landscape, 1865. From the text of *De la Terre à la Lune*: a massive cannon, 274 meters long, cast into the earth at Stone's Hill in the Florida peninsula. The projectile — the Columbiad — is a hollow aluminum cylinder, conical at the nose, carrying three passengers in a pressurized interior. Crowds gather to watch the launch. Searchlights sweep the sky. The ground shakes when the charge fires. The camera moves slowly along the length of the cannon barrel embedded in the earth — a piece of engineering that does not yet exist and will not exist for a century — as night sky and searchlight beams sweep overhead.

Key Takeaway

Jules Verne published *From the Earth to the Moon* in 1865. In it, three men were launched from the Florida peninsula in an aluminum capsule called the Columbiad, splashed down in the Pacific, and were recovered by the U.S. Navy. One hundred and four years later, NASA launched Apollo 11 from Cape Canaveral, Florida — three astronauts in an aluminum alloy capsule called Columbia — and recovered it from the Pacific. Verne chose Florida because he calculated it correctly: the equatorial geometry made it the optimal launch latitude. NASA chose it for the same reason. The fiction preceded the engineering by a century, and the engineering arrived at the same answer.
