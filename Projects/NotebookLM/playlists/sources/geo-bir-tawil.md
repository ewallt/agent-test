# Bir Tawil: The Land No Country Wants

Bir Tawil is a 2,060-square-kilometer patch of desert in northeast Africa, wedged between Egypt and Sudan, that neither country claims as its own. It is one of the only inhabited places on earth — outside Antarctica — that belongs to no nation. Both Egypt and Sudan want the other country to have it. Their reasons involve a more valuable piece of land nearby, and a colonial boundary decision made in 1902 that created a contradiction no one has resolved in over a century.

Scene 1 — The Unclaimed Desert

The camera holds on a flat desert landscape — ochre sand, low scrub, a distant ridge of dark hills under a pale sky. No roads. No buildings. No flag. No border marker visible in any direction. This is Bir Tawil: 2,060 square kilometers of desert between Egypt and Sudan that neither country claims. In a world where every piece of habitable land has been parceled out among nations, where borders are contested, wars are fought over them, and sovereignty is one of the things people die defending — Bir Tawil is the exception. Both neighboring countries are actively declining to own it. The camera pulls back slowly to show the full emptiness of the place.

Scene 2 — Two Borders, One Contradiction

In 1899, Britain drew a straight line across the map at the 22nd parallel to separate Egypt from Sudan — both under British control. In 1902, the same British administration drew a different line for administrative purposes, one that followed tribal territories rather than the parallel. The two lines created two anomalies: a small area north of the 1899 line administered by Sudan (the Hala'ib Triangle, on the Red Sea coast), and a small area south of the 1899 line administered by Egypt (Bir Tawil, inland desert). The camera pushes in on a colonial-era map on a table — two lines are drawn across it in different ink. A hand traces the contradiction between them. Neither line matches the other.

SETTING: A colonial administration office, early 1900s — a map of northeast Africa
SUBJECT: A colonial-era map of the Egypt-Sudan border showing the two conflicting boundary lines
IN-SCENE MOTION: A hand tracing each line in turn; documents shifting on the desk
LIGHTING: Desk lamp on paper; the map lit at its center; official room beyond in shadow
NARRATION BEAT: The bureaucratic origin of the anomaly — two boundaries that contradict each other

---

Scene 3 — Why Nobody Wants It

Bir Tawil is 2,060 square kilometers of desert with no permanent population, no mineral resources of known value, no agricultural potential, and no coastline. The Hala'ib Triangle — the other anomaly created by the same contradiction — is 20,580 square kilometers, has a Red Sea coastline, fishing grounds, and some mineral deposits. If Egypt claims Bir Tawil under the 1902 administrative line, it must also accept that the Hala'ib Triangle belongs to Sudan. If Sudan claims Bir Tawil under the same logic, it loses the Hala'ib Triangle to Egypt. Both countries have chosen the Hala'ib Triangle. The price of that choice is leaving Bir Tawil unclaimed. The camera holds on the desert. The trade was worth it. Nobody wants this.

SETTING: Bir Tawil — the desert landscape from a slight elevation, showing the full flat terrain
SUBJECT: The featureless desert of Bir Tawil stretching to a distant ridge
IN-SCENE MOTION: Heat shimmer rising from the desert floor; a single bird crossing at the edge of frame
LIGHTING: Bright afternoon sun; harsh white sky; long flat shadows from low scrub
NARRATION BEAT: The logic of the trade — Hala'ib versus Bir Tawil, and why both countries made the same choice

Scene 4 — The Men Who Tried to Claim It

Bir Tawil has attracted a small and persistent category of person: those who want to found a country. In 2014, a Virginia man named Jeremiah Heaton traveled to Bir Tawil, planted a flag he designed himself, and declared it the Kingdom of North Sudan — so his daughter could be a real princess. He received media coverage. He did not receive diplomatic recognition. Since then, others have planted flags, written constitutions, and issued press releases from Bir Tawil. None of it has produced a recognized state. The camera holds on a lone figure in the desert, a hand-sewn flag planted in the sand beside them, the horizon empty in every direction. The gesture is sincere. The result is the same.

SUBJECT: A person standing beside a flag planted in the sand — the desert stretching to the horizon
IN-SCENE MOTION: The flag fabric moving in the desert wind; sand shifting at its base
LIGHTING: Bright midday desert sun; the figure in strong shadow on one side; sky white behind
NARRATION BEAT: The would-be claimants — and why the gesture cannot become a nation

Scene 5 — What Sovereignty Requires

A country requires more than a flag in the sand. It requires recognition from other countries, a government capable of making binding agreements, a permanent population, and the practical ability to administer the territory. Bir Tawil has no permanent population and no infrastructure. More importantly, it has no value — which is precisely why no established government will claim it. Sovereignty over Bir Tawil costs a country the Hala'ib Triangle. No unrecognized claimant has the standing to give either country anything in return. The camera tracks slowly along the empty desert horizon. The flags planted here have all blown away or faded. The desert has not changed.

SETTING: Bir Tawil — the desert landscape at dusk, the horizon turning amber
SUBJECT: The featureless desert — no flags, no structures, the light changing
IN-SCENE MOTION: Wind moving across sand; the color of the light shifting as the sun lowers
LIGHTING: Late afternoon golden light raking across the sand; long shadows from rocks; sky deepening
NARRATION BEAT: Why flag-planting cannot produce sovereignty — and what actually creates a country

---

Scene 6 — The Useful Exception

Bir Tawil is not entirely useless. It is a useful illustration: it shows what happens when two colonial boundary decisions contradict each other and no one corrects the error. It shows what sovereignty actually costs — not the act of claiming something, but the willingness to accept the obligations and tradeoffs that ownership creates. And it shows that the world's map is nearly complete, that almost every piece of land has been assigned and fought over, with one small exception: a flat piece of desert where both neighbors quietly agree to look the other way. The camera pulls back from the desert at dusk until only the sky is visible, the horizon a thin dark line below.

SETTING: Bir Tawil at dusk — wide aerial view, the desert in shadow, sky still lit
SUBJECT: The unclaimed territory from above — its outline faint, the surrounding desert identical
IN-SCENE MOTION: Slow aerial pull-back until the horizon fills the frame below a wide sky
LIGHTING: Dusk — warm amber at the horizon, deep blue above; the land below losing definition
NARRATION BEAT: What the exception reveals — about sovereignty, about colonialism, about the cost of ownership

---

Key Takeaway

Bir Tawil exists as an unclaimed territory because both Egypt and Sudan want a more valuable piece of land on the Red Sea coast. To claim Bir Tawil, either country would have to accept the 1902 administrative line — and that line gives the coast to the other. The unclaimed desert is not an oversight. It is the deliberate result of two countries making the same rational calculation. The world has very few places that belong to no one. This is one of them, and the reason why tells you something about what belonging actually means.
