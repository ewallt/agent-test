# Edgar Degas: The Dancer of Modern Life

Edgar Degas called himself a Realist. He disliked the word "Impressionist" and bristled at being grouped with painters who worked outdoors in natural light. He almost never did. He painted in his studio, from memory and from obsessive preliminary sketches, constructing images that looked spontaneous but were the product of relentless calculation. In this way Degas was the movement's great paradox — the most modern of them all, and the least typical.

## The Fixation on Movement

Degas's subject was motion caught in the act: the ballerina mid-rehearsal, the jockey on a turning horse, the laundress bent over her iron, the café singer mid-phrase. He was not interested in the posed figure. He wanted the figure doing something — off-balance, caught from an unexpected angle, unaware of being watched.

The dancer was his obsession for nearly forty years. He produced more than 700 works on the subject — paintings, pastels, drawings, bronze sculptures. But his dancers were not the idealized figures of classical ballet imagery. They were working women: young, often poor, stretching sore muscles, adjusting straps, waiting in the wings. He painted them from the orchestra pit, from the wings, from above. He painted them with the overhead lighting that made shadows fall upward, distorting faces into something almost unsettling. The glamour was intentional and deceptive at the same time.

## The New Tools: Pastel and Photography

Two technologies shaped Degas's mature work. The first was pastel. He used it not as a secondary medium but as his primary one — building up layers of color with a cross-hatching technique that gave his surfaces a vibrating, textured quality no oil painting could replicate. His late pastels, made when his eyesight was failing, are among the most radical works of the nineteenth century — color pushed to near-abstraction, the figure barely held together by light.

The second was photography. Degas was deeply interested in the work of Eadweard Muybridge, whose sequential photographs of horses in motion (1878) proved that painters had always been wrong about how horses galloped. No horse runs the way academic painters had depicted it. Degas incorporated this knowledge — his horses have the angular, mid-stride quality of photographs. More broadly, he absorbed photography's way of cropping: figures cut off at the edge of the canvas, compositions that felt accidental but were carefully designed to feel that way.

## Paris as Subject

Degas's Paris was not the sunlit countryside of Monet or the riverside pleasures of Renoir. It was the city at work and at play — the café-concert, the racetrack, the opera, the laundry, the brothel. His series of women bathing, produced in the 1880s and 1890s, scandalized critics not because they were erotic but because they weren't. Women crouching in tin tubs, drying their backs, combing their hair — ordinary bodies performing private acts, observed without sentiment or titillation. Degas said he wanted to show the human animal attending to itself, "as if you were looking through a keyhole."

## The Difficult Man

Degas was famously difficult. He quarreled with nearly everyone, ended friendships abruptly, and became in his later years increasingly bitter. During the Dreyfus Affair — the crisis that split France over the false conviction of a Jewish army officer — Degas declared himself a fierce anti-Dreyfusard, ending lifelong friendships with Jewish artists and patrons. His politics were reactionary, his personality prickly, his solitude increasingly complete.

None of this diminishes what he made. The works survive their maker, as they always do.

## What He Left Behind

Degas never married, rarely sold his work, and died in 1917 leaving a studio crammed with nearly everything he had ever made — kept back from the world, hoarded, refined. The estate sale took three weeks.

What he left was a body of work that changed how artists thought about composition, angle, and the relationship between observation and construction. The cropped frame, the oblique view, the figure in action rather than in pose — these became the grammar of modern art. Degas arrived at them before anyone else understood why they mattered.
