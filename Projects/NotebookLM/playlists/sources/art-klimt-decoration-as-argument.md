# Decoration as Argument

Gustav Klimt was trained as a decorative muralist — the kind of craftsman who painted grand ceilings for the Viennese establishment. He ended up leading the revolt against that establishment. The Secession, the scandal of his University paintings, the gold-leaf canvases: all of it was one sustained argument — that decoration is not lesser than fine art, that ornament is not the enemy of meaning. Vienna disagreed loudly, and Klimt never changed his position.

---

Scene 1 — The Commission

A vast interior — a university hall under construction, scaffolding filling the space. High on a platform, workers are applying preliminary sketches to a ceiling section. Below, officials in formal dress inspect a maquette. One points at something. Another shakes his head. The camera cranes up slowly from the officials to the ceiling, then holds on the blank space waiting to be filled.

SETTING: Vienna University, 1894 — early stages of the ceiling commission
SUBJECT: The official commission that would become a scandal
IN-SCENE MOTION: Officials examine the maquette below; workers move on scaffolding above; the camera cranes up
LIGHTING: Construction interior light — cold, unfinished; shafts from high windows
NARRATION BEAT: In 1894, Klimt received the prestigious commission to paint allegorical murals for the University of Vienna's ceiling — Philosophy, Medicine, Jurisprudence. The Faculty expected classical allegory. What they got was something else entirely.

---

Scene 2 — The Scandal

A formal presentation hall. A large canvas is unveiled before an audience of professors and officials. The image shows writhing human bodies — naked, intertwined, suspended — no clear allegory, no triumphant figure, no reassuring resolution. The audience is silent for a moment, then the murmur begins. The camera holds on the unveiled painting, not on the audience.

SETTING: Vienna, 1900 — public unveiling of Philosophy
SUBJECT: The Faculty's reaction to what Klimt actually painted
IN-SCENE MOTION: The canvas is revealed; the audience murmur builds; no one approaches the painting
LIGHTING: Formal presentation lighting; the painting is lit from above
NARRATION BEAT: Eighty-seven professors signed a protest. The works were called pornographic, nihilistic, perverse. Klimt had been asked to represent knowledge triumphing over darkness. He had painted the human body adrift in the infinite — unrescued by reason.

---

Scene 3 — The Secession

A new building under construction in Vienna — clean geometric lines, a golden dome in the shape of laurel leaves, the inscription above the door: "To every age its art, to art its freedom." Workers lay the final stones. The camera holds on the inscription, then slowly pulls back to show the whole facade.

SETTING: Vienna Secession building, Naschmarkt, 1897–1898
SUBJECT: The founding of the breakaway artists' group
IN-SCENE MOTION: Final construction details being completed; the inscription is set into place
LIGHTING: Clear day, the gilded dome catching direct sunlight
NARRATION BEAT: In 1897, Klimt co-founded the Vienna Secession — a breakaway from the official artists' association. The new building's motto said plainly what they were after: art free from academic authority.

---

Scene 4 — The Gold

A studio, afternoon. Klimt applies gold leaf to a canvas with a fine brush, working in small sections. The gold catches the light as he lays it down — shifting, warm, Byzantine. The camera is close on his hands and the canvas surface. Behind him, on the wall, Byzantine mosaics pinned for reference. The room is quiet except for the faint sound of the gold being pressed flat.

SETTING: Klimt's Vienna studio, early 1900s
SUBJECT: Klimt working with gold leaf — his most distinctive technical innovation
IN-SCENE MOTION: His hands apply the gold leaf in small careful sections; it catches the light
LIGHTING: Studio light at an angle that makes the gold surface shift and glow
NARRATION BEAT: Klimt had seen Byzantine mosaics in Ravenna in 1903 and understood immediately: flatness and gold were not limitations — they were arguments. Against illusionism. Against the idea that painting must pretend to be a window.

---

Scene 5 — The Kiss

A gold canvas, close. Two figures — a man and a woman — wrapped together in a field of gold and flowers, the border between them and the gold background barely visible. The man's face is in profile; the woman's eyes are closed. The camera moves very slowly across the surface of the painting, from edge to edge, close enough to see the texture of the gold leaf.

SETTING: Klimt's studio, 1907–1908
SUBJECT: The Kiss being completed
IN-SCENE MOTION: The camera tracks slowly across the surface of the painting; the gold shifts as the angle changes
LIGHTING: Studio light raking across the canvas at a low angle; the gold glows
NARRATION BEAT: The Kiss was finished in 1908. It is almost entirely flat — the figures dissolve into their golden ground. It is also the most intimate painting he ever made. The flatness and the intimacy are not in tension. They are the same thing.

---

Scene 6 — The Refusal

An official building, Vienna, early 1900s. A clerk extends an envelope across a desk — an official letter with a red seal. On the other side of the desk, Klimt looks at it briefly, then shakes his head and leaves without taking it. The camera stays on the envelope on the desk as his footsteps recede.

SETTING: An official Vienna building, early 1900s
SUBJECT: Klimt declining official recognition
IN-SCENE MOTION: He looks at the envelope, shakes his head, turns and walks out; the envelope stays on the desk
LIGHTING: Interior administrative light; grey and institutional
NARRATION BEAT: Klimt refused the Emperor's Prize. He declined a professorship at the Academy of Fine Arts. He gave no interviews and almost no public statements. His position was entirely in the work.

---

Key Takeaway

Klimt's argument was that decoration and meaning are not opposites — that a surface can be both beautiful and serious, both ornamental and true. Vienna called it pornography. The rest of the world called it The Kiss.
