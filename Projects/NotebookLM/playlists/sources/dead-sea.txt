The Dead Sea — The Lowest Place on Earth Is Getting Lower

The Dead Sea sits at 430 meters below sea level — the lowest point on the surface of the earth. Its water is ten times saltier than the ocean. Nothing lives in it. People float in it without trying, reading newspapers, lying on their backs in the sun. For centuries it has been considered a curiosity and a wonder. In the last fifty years it has been disappearing. The water level drops more than a meter every year. And as it drops, the ground around it is collapsing.

Scene 1 — The Surface

A flat, still expanse of water — the color of pale turquoise fading to white at the edges where salt has crystallized on the shore. No waves. No wind disturbing the surface. The camera looks out across the Dead Sea from the Jordanian shore in the early morning, the water perfectly still, the Israeli hills on the other side in blue haze.

SETTING: The Dead Sea, Jordanian shore, early morning
SUBJECT: The surface of the water — still, saline, otherworldly in color
IN-SCENE MOTION: A single ripple moves slowly across the foreground; the camera holds
LIGHTING: Early morning, the sun not yet high; the water catches a pale gold; the salt crust at the shore is white
NARRATION BEAT: The water is so dense with salt that it is almost a liquid solid. It supports a person's weight the way ordinary water supports a boat.

Scene 2 — The Bather

A man walks into the Dead Sea — knee deep, hip deep. He leans back and his feet rise. He does not swim. He floats on his back as if lying on a firm mattress, his arms out, his head above water. He holds a folded newspaper open above him and reads. The camera holds in a wide shot — the floating man, the still water, the hills.

SETTING: The Dead Sea shallows, midday
SUBJECT: A person floating effortlessly on the surface
IN-SCENE MOTION: The man turns a page of the newspaper; his body bobs very slightly; no other motion
LIGHTING: Harsh midday sun; the water reflects white; the man's newspaper is too bright to read in this light, but he reads anyway
NARRATION BEAT: The Dead Sea is 34% salinity — compared to 3.5% in the ocean. The density makes floating automatic. The challenge is not staying up. The challenge is getting your feet back down.

Scene 3 — The Salt Formations

A section of shoreline. The water has receded and left behind strange white formations — angular salt crystals encrusting the rocks, coating whatever was submerged: a rusted pipe, a wooden post, the underside of a dock. The camera tracks slowly along the formation, close enough to see the crystal structure.

SETTING: The Dead Sea shoreline — exposed salt formations
SUBJECT: Salt crystals encrusting abandoned objects at the former waterline
IN-SCENE MOTION: The camera tracks along the formations; a small piece of encrusted wood shifts; salt flakes fall
LIGHTING: Direct sun, late morning; the salt crystals are intensely white; everything around them is in shadow by comparison
NARRATION BEAT: The Dead Sea deposits salt on everything it touches. The formations are beautiful and they are evidence — the waterline that produced them is now several meters above the current surface.

Scene 4 — The Old Resort

A concrete building at the edge of the desert — a resort hotel from the 1960s. Behind it, a long slope of cracked mud and salt flat stretching down to the water's edge. The swimming pool is empty. The dock extends from the building into empty air — the water is now 300 meters away. The camera slowly pans from the building across the flat expanse of exposed lakebed to the current shoreline.

SETTING: An abandoned Dead Sea resort, Israeli shore
SUBJECT: The gap between where the resort was built and where the water now is
IN-SCENE MOTION: The camera pans slowly from the building across the exposed lakebed; dust blows across the foreground
LIGHTING: Afternoon haze; the salt flat is blindingly pale; the sky is washed out
NARRATION BEAT: The Dead Sea drops more than a meter every year. Resorts built at the water's edge in the 1970s are now hundreds of meters from the shore. The dock from this hotel has been extending into open air since 1985.

Scene 5 — The Sinkhole

A road in the Jordan Valley. A section of asphalt has simply dropped — a neat circular hole, three meters across, two meters deep, in the middle of a lane. The edges are clean. There is no rubble. The camera holds above the sinkhole, looking down, then pulls back to show the road and the landscape.

SETTING: A road near the Dead Sea, Jordan Valley — a sinkhole
SUBJECT: A perfectly formed sinkhole in a road
IN-SCENE MOTION: A small stone rolls to the edge and drops in; the camera pulls back slowly
LIGHTING: Midday sun; the sinkhole is in deep shadow; the surrounding asphalt is hot and bright
NARRATION BEAT: As the Dead Sea shrinks, the fresh water table follows it down. The water dissolves ancient underground salt deposits. The ground above collapses without warning — on roads, in parking lots, in farmers' fields, on beaches. There are now more than 6,000 sinkholes around the Dead Sea.

Scene 6 — The Jordan River

A narrow stream moves through reeds and willows — this is the Jordan River, the Dead Sea's only significant inflow. The water is slow and shallow. The camera holds on the river from the bank, the flow barely visible.

SETTING: The lower Jordan River, near the Dead Sea inflow
SUBJECT: What remains of the river that once fed the sea
IN-SCENE MOTION: The shallow current moves through the reeds; a bird moves in the background
LIGHTING: Afternoon dappled light through the willows; the water is pale green
NARRATION BEAT: The Jordan River once delivered 1.3 billion cubic meters of water per year to the Dead Sea. Today it delivers less than 3% of that. Israel, Jordan, and Syria have diverted almost all of it for agriculture and drinking water. What reaches the sea is mostly agricultural runoff.

Scene 7 — The High Water Line

A cliff face beside the Dead Sea. On the rock, a dark horizontal stain — a mineral deposit left by the old waterline, now visibly above the current surface. The camera holds in a steady medium shot on the cliff face, the line and the current water level both visible.

SETTING: A cliff face along the Dead Sea shore
SUBJECT: The old high water line marked on the rock
IN-SCENE MOTION: The camera slowly tilts from the current water surface up the cliff face to the old waterline
LIGHTING: Late afternoon; the cliff face in shadow; the water below in sunlight
NARRATION BEAT: The Dead Sea has been falling since the 1960s. The high water marks on the cliffs record where the surface used to be. The distance from the old mark to the water below is not geological time — it is living memory.

Scene 8 — The Projection

A simple graph on a screen — the Dead Sea water level over time. A line descends steadily from left to right. A dotted extension continues the line. The camera holds on the screen as the dotted line continues into the future, descending.

SETTING: A research office — a graph on a monitor
SUBJECT: The projected trajectory of the Dead Sea water level
IN-SCENE MOTION: The dotted line extends, frame by frame, into the future; the camera holds
LIGHTING: Screen light in a dim room
NARRATION BEAT: At current rates, the Dead Sea will drop another 30 meters by the end of this century. There is a Red Sea–Dead Sea Canal project that has been discussed, proposed, and partially funded for decades. It has not been built. The water level does not wait.

Key Takeaway
The Dead Sea is dying of thirst in the middle of a desert. The paradox is that the water that made it remarkable — its extreme salinity, its density, its otherworldly surface — is precisely what makes it so fragile. It has no outlet. It survives entirely on inflow. And for sixty years, the inflow has been diverted. The sinkholes opening around its shore are the ground registering a loss that maps and graphs have recorded for decades but that the eye can only see when the earth itself gives way.
