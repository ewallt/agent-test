# The Failed Wallpaper: How Bubble Wrap Found a Use on Its Third Try

Alfred Fielding and Marc Chavannes were engineers in Hawthorne, New Jersey in 1957 trying to make a new kind of textured wallpaper. They sealed two shower curtains together using a heat-sealing machine and trapped air between them in small regular pockets. The wallpaper failed — no one wanted plastic bubbles on their walls. They tried to sell it as greenhouse insulation. That failed too. Three years later, IBM used it to protect its new 1401 computer during shipping. Bubble Wrap had finally found a job, on its third attempt, in a context its inventors had never imagined.

---

Scene 1 — The Shower Curtains

A garage workshop, Hawthorne, New Jersey, 1957. Alfred Fielding and Marc Chavannes are running two shower curtains through a heat-sealing machine, pressing them together with small regular gaps to trap air. They are trying to make a wallpaper with texture and depth — something modern, three-dimensional, different from the flat printed patterns on the market. The camera pulls back slowly from the machine to show the full workspace — the curtains feeding through, the sealed rows of air bubbles emerging — a product being born that will never be used for its intended purpose.

Scene 2 — The Wallpaper Fails

A product showroom or sales office, 1957–1958. Fielding and Chavannes present their textured plastic wallpaper to potential buyers. The reaction is the same each time: polite, puzzled, uninterested. People do not want plastic bubbles on their walls. The product does not fit any established category of home furnishing. The camera tracks slowly along the sample boards on the wall — the regular grid of air pockets, visually distinctive, commercially useless — as the room empties and Fielding and Chavannes are left with their invention and no market for it.

Scene 3 — The Greenhouse Pitch

An agricultural supply context, 1959. Fielding and Chavannes reposition the material as insulation for greenhouses: the trapped air provides thermal buffering, the plastic is durable, the product is practical. They make the rounds of horticultural suppliers. The pitch fails again. Greenhouse operators already have insulation methods; the new material offers no compelling advantage at its price point. The camera holds on a sample of the material in a buyer's hands — turning it over, pressing a bubble, setting it down — then cuts to the empty order form on the desk.

Scene 4 — IBM Ships a Computer

Endicott, New York, 1960. IBM is preparing to ship its new 1401 data processing system — a large, fragile, expensive machine. Someone in the logistics chain selects Fielding and Chavannes's bubble material as protective packaging. The air pockets absorb shock; the plastic is clean and consistent; the material wraps easily around irregular equipment. The camera follows a pallet being wrapped — the 1401's panels disappearing under a layer of bubbles — then tracks the crated machine onto a loading dock. The failed wallpaper has found its first real job.

Scene 5 — Sealed Air Corporation

A manufacturing facility, early 1960s. Fielding and Chavannes found Sealed Air Corporation in 1960, pivoting entirely to the packaging application. Orders grow as the logic spreads: any fragile object that must survive shipping needs what bubble wrap provides. The camera moves through the factory floor — rolls of the material being produced, cut, shipped — the same grid of air pockets that failed as wallpaper and greenhouse insulation moving through the world now at industrial scale, in a context the inventors did not imagine in 1957.

Scene 6 — The Pop

A shipping box, any year after 1960. Someone pulls an object from a box and sets aside the protective wrapping. Then, almost without thinking, begins pressing the bubbles — one, then another, the small percussive pop. The camera holds in close on the fingers working across the sheet, the rhythmic destruction of the air pockets that were once supposed to insulate a greenhouse. Bubble Wrap is now made in enough volume each year to circle the Earth more than ten times. The camera pulls back slowly to show the full sheet of it — the grid of bubbles, most of them still intact — and the hands moving steadily through it.

---

Key Takeaway

Fielding and Chavannes invented Bubble Wrap in 1957 trying to make textured wallpaper. When that failed, they tried to sell it as greenhouse insulation. When that failed, IBM used it to ship a computer. The material did not change between its first failure and its eventual success — only the context changed. Bubble Wrap found its purpose on its third attempt, in an application that had nothing to do with why it was invented.
