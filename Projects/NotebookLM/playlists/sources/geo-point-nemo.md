# Point Nemo: The Most Alone Place on Earth

Point Nemo is the oceanic pole of inaccessibility — the single point in the ocean farthest from any land on earth. It sits in the South Pacific, 2,688 kilometers from the nearest coastline in any direction. The astronauts aboard the International Space Station are often closer to it than any human being on the surface. It is where space agencies send dead satellites to fall. It is where nothing lives. And it is one of the strangest addresses on the planet.

---

Scene 1 — Nothing in Every Direction

The camera holds at sea level in the open South Pacific — grey swells moving under an overcast sky, no land visible anywhere, no ships, no birds. Just ocean to the horizon in every direction. This is not simply remote. This is the furthest from land any point in the ocean can be: 2,688 kilometers to the nearest coast, in any direction. The nearest land is not a continent — it is a tiny island. The nearest humans, on most days, are not on earth at all. They are passing overhead at 400 kilometers altitude aboard the International Space Station. The camera holds on the empty ocean. Nothing moves except the water.

Scene 2 — How You Find the Middle of Nothing

The oceanic pole of inaccessibility was calculated in 1992 by a Croatian-Canadian engineer named Hrvoje Lukatela. He used geospatial software to find the single point in the global ocean maximally equidistant from all coastlines. The answer came back as a set of coordinates in the South Pacific: 48 degrees 52 minutes south, 123 degrees 23 minutes west. The three nearest land masses are Ducie Island in the Pitcairn group, Motu Nui off Easter Island, and the Antarctic coast. All of them are exactly 2,688 kilometers away. The camera pushes in on a paper map on a desk, a pencil marking the point — an X in the middle of nothing.

SUBJECT: A hand marking coordinates on a nautical chart of the South Pacific
IN-SCENE MOTION: A pencil marking a precise point on the map; fingers measuring with a ruler
LIGHTING: Desk lamp on paper; soft room light beyond
NARRATION BEAT: Who found it and how — the calculation behind the point

Scene 3 — The Spacecraft Cemetery

Space agencies have been deliberately crashing dead satellites and spacecraft into Point Nemo for decades. It is the safest place to aim a falling object: the ocean is deep, there are no shipping lanes, no fisheries, no populated coastlines within 2,688 kilometers. The Russian Mir space station splashed down here in 2001. More than 300 spacecraft have been sent to the same address. The camera holds on a night sky, a bright streak of re-entry light scoring across the southern stars — a satellite becoming a fireball, then fragments, then nothing. Somewhere below, the wreckage settles into the deep. The ocean accepts it without event.

SETTING: Open South Pacific at night — dark sky, southern stars, the ocean below
SUBJECT: A spacecraft re-entry trail crossing the sky above Point Nemo
IN-SCENE MOTION: A bright streak moving across the frame from upper right to lower left; fragmenting near the end
LIGHTING: Deep dark sky; southern constellations visible; the re-entry trail the only light
NARRATION BEAT: Why Point Nemo is a spacecraft graveyard — and the scale of what has been deposited there

Scene 4 — The Dead Zone

Point Nemo is also one of the least biologically productive ocean zones on earth. The South Pacific Gyre — a slow circular current surrounding the region — acts as a barrier, preventing the nutrient-rich waters of other ocean zones from reaching it. There is little to eat here, which means almost nothing lives here. Deep below the surface the camera descends slowly through water so clear it is almost colorless — no fish, no jellyfish, no visible plankton. Just cold, clear, empty water descending toward a floor no one has mapped in detail. The most remote place and one of the most biologically empty: two kinds of nothing at once.

SETTING: Underwater at Point Nemo — open ocean midwater column, no seabed visible
SUBJECT: The deep ocean water column — clear, empty, descending into darkness
IN-SCENE MOTION: Slow downward camera drift; particles drifting upward; no fauna visible
LIGHTING: Faint surface light dimming to deep blue-grey; no bioluminescence
NARRATION BEAT: The biological emptiness — the gyre barrier and what it means for life here

Scene 5 — Cthulhu

In 1997, NOAA hydrophones detected an extremely powerful ultra-low-frequency sound emanating from the South Pacific — louder than any known biological source. They named it the Bloop. Its origin was debated for years. Some attributed it to an unknown deep-sea creature. In 2012, NOAA concluded it was almost certainly the sound of a large icequake — Antarctic ice cracking and shifting on the ocean floor. The source of the Bloop was located near Point Nemo. H.P. Lovecraft, writing in 1928, had placed the sunken city of R'lyeh — home of the sleeping monster Cthulhu — at coordinates that fall within a few hundred kilometers of Point Nemo. He had chosen the coordinates by finding the most remote ocean location on a map. He was not far off. The camera holds on the dark ocean surface at night. Below it, nothing answers.

SUBJECT: The surface of the water at Point Nemo — dark, still between swells, lit only by moonlight
IN-SCENE MOTION: Slow swells moving across the frame; moonlight shifting on the water
LIGHTING: Moonlight on the water; deep darkness below the surface; stars overhead
NARRATION BEAT: The Bloop, Lovecraft, and the mythology that has attached itself to the emptiest address on earth

Scene 6 — The Address

Point Nemo has no permanent human presence — no station, no buoy, no anchor. Ships do not pass through it intentionally. It exists only as a coordinate: an answer to the question of where on the surface of the ocean you could be most alone. The camera holds at sea level one last time as the swell moves through frame. A single albatross crosses far in the distance, white against the grey sky, and disappears. The ocean continues. There is no marker here. There never was.

SUBJECT: The ocean surface at Point Nemo — water, sky, and a single distant albatross
IN-SCENE MOTION: A lone albatross crossing the far background; swells moving through; wind on the water surface
LIGHTING: Flat overcast light; grey-white sky meeting grey-green water at the horizon
NARRATION BEAT: The final image — what it means to be the most remote place on earth, and why no one stays

Key Takeaway

Point Nemo is the oceanic pole of inaccessibility: the single point on earth where you are farthest from land in every direction. The astronauts on the ISS are often the closest humans to it. Space agencies crash dead satellites there because nothing else is. Its nearest literary neighbor is a Lovecraft story written in 1928. Its coordinates answer a question that has no practical use — and that is why people keep asking it.
