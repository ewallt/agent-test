# Andrew Wiles and Fermat's Last Theorem: The Last Morning

In 1637, Pierre de Fermat scribbled a note in the margin of a mathematics book claiming he had proved something remarkable — that no whole numbers could satisfy a simple equation beyond a certain power. "I have a truly marvelous proof," he wrote, "which this margin is too narrow to contain." He died without writing it down. For 358 years, the greatest mathematicians in the world tried and failed to find it. Then a ten-year-old boy in Cambridge picked up a library book about the problem — and decided, on the spot, that he would be the one to solve it.

---

Scene 1 — The Boy and the Problem

A ten-year-old sits alone at a library table, a slim book open in front of him. The afternoon light comes in low through tall windows, falling across the table in long bars. He leans forward to read. The camera pushes in slowly from the far end of the room, past empty chairs and shelves of books, until the boy's face fills the frame — utterly absorbed, the library dissolving behind him. He is not reading for school. He has found something.

SETTING: A public library in Cambridge, England, 1963
SUBJECT: Andrew Wiles, age ten, reading "The Last Problem" by Eric Temple Bell
IN-SCENE MOTION: Boy leaning in, turning pages slowly; dust motes drifting in library light
LIGHTING: Late afternoon light through tall library windows, long shadows across the reading table
NARRATION BEAT: Fermat's Last Theorem — 358 years old, never proved, famous for its simplicity and its stubbornness

---

Scene 2 — The Secret Years

A small attic room in Princeton. A desk beneath a single window. The walls are covered in pinned pages — equations, diagrams, lines crossed out and rewritten. Notebooks are stacked in columns along the wall. A man sits at the desk with his back to the camera. He does not look up. The camera tracks slowly from left to right across the room, taking in the full breadth of the work — years of it, accumulated in the silence of a room no one else enters.

SETTING: Andrew Wiles's attic study, Princeton University, late 1980s–1993
SUBJECT: Wiles at his desk, alone, surrounded by seven years of secret work
IN-SCENE MOTION: Wiles writing; papers moving slightly in the draft from the window; a cup of coffee steaming on the desk
LIGHTING: Flat grey morning light from a single attic window; the room slightly dim
NARRATION BEAT: Wiles tells no one. He pursues a back-door route — if a conjecture called Taniyama-Shimura can be proved for a certain class of equations, Fermat's theorem follows as a consequence. He works alone for seven years.

---

Scene 3 — The First Lecture

A lecture hall at Cambridge. A few dozen mathematicians in the audience — a normal-sized crowd for a specialist seminar. Wiles stands at the blackboard and begins to speak. The camera holds at the back of the room in a wide static shot: the audience seated, the speaker at the board, the ordinary furniture of mathematical discourse. Nothing unusual. But a few people in the front rows begin to lean forward slightly, exchanging a glance.

SETTING: Isaac Newton Institute, Cambridge, June 1993 — first of three lectures
SUBJECT: Wiles lecturing, a sparse audience behind him
IN-SCENE MOTION: Wiles writing on the board; audience members shifting in their seats; a few heads tilting toward each other
LIGHTING: Institutional fluorescent light; warm tones from old wood paneling
NARRATION BEAT: Wiles is proving the Taniyama-Shimura conjecture for semistable elliptic curves. To most in the room, it looks like a lecture on a difficult but narrow problem. A few mathematicians begin to see where it's going.

---

Scene 4 — The Third Lecture

The same hall — but now it is packed. Every seat taken, people standing along the walls and in the aisles. Word spread between the first and third lectures. A handheld camera moves through the standing crowd at the back, taking in the faces — concentration, barely suppressed excitement, one mathematician leaning to whisper to another. At the front of the room, Wiles writes the final line on the board, sets down the chalk, and turns to face the room. He says: "I think I'll stop here."

SETTING: Isaac Newton Institute, Cambridge, June 23, 1993 — third lecture
SUBJECT: Wiles at the board; a packed, standing-room crowd
IN-SCENE MOTION: Wiles writing; audience members leaning forward; whispering at the back of the room; chalk settling in the tray
LIGHTING: Warm lecture hall light; faces lit from the front, the back of the room in soft shadow
NARRATION BEAT: The room understands what has just happened. For the first time in 358 years, someone may have proved Fermat's Last Theorem. The announcement makes international news within hours.

---

Scene 5 — The Flaw

A quiet office. Autumn afternoon light. Wiles sits at a desk, reading a letter. The camera holds in a medium shot — his face lit from the side by the window, steady, concentrated. He sets the letter down on the desk. The camera slowly pushes in on his hands, resting flat on the paper. Outside the window, ordinary light continues as if nothing has changed.

SETTING: Wiles's Princeton office, September 1993
SUBJECT: Wiles reading a letter from a colleague who has found an error in the proof
IN-SCENE MOTION: Wiles setting the letter down; hands still on the desk
LIGHTING: Pale autumn afternoon light from the window, falling at a low angle across the desk
NARRATION BEAT: A reviewer has found a gap in the argument. A section involving something called the Euler system — the heart of one of the proof's key mechanisms — doesn't hold. Wiles expects to fix it quickly.

---

Scene 6 — A Year Passes

The attic room again. The camera holds in a wide, still shot of the room — the same desk, the same window. Over the scene, light changes: the sharp summer light of one season bleeds into the grey of the next. The desk fills and clears and fills again. The stacks shift. The man at the desk grows quieter, more still. The camera never moves. The room is the same. Something in it is not.

SETTING: Wiles's attic study, Princeton, September 1993 — September 1994
SUBJECT: The room itself — the desk, the window, the accumulation of failed attempts
IN-SCENE MOTION: Wiles writing, stopping, starting over; seasonal light shifting across the attic floor
LIGHTING: Moving through seasons — summer warmth, then pale autumn, then grey winter, then cold spring
NARRATION BEAT: The quick fix takes a year. Wiles tries every approach he can construct. Every one fails. He comes close to asking for help. He almost announces the proof is dead.

---

Scene 7 — The Last Morning

A Monday in September, 1994. Morning light in the attic. Wiles sits at the desk preparing to write the letter that will publicly withdraw the proof. He pauses. He reaches for an old notebook — the Iwasawa theory work he had attempted years before and abandoned, certain it wasn't powerful enough. He opens it. He begins to read. The camera pushes in slowly on his hands, turning pages. His hands go still.

SETTING: Wiles's attic study, Princeton, September 19, 1994
SUBJECT: Wiles reading the abandoned Iwasawa notebook
IN-SCENE MOTION: Wiles turning pages slowly, then going still; early morning light moving across the desk
LIGHTING: Soft, cool morning light from the attic window; the room beginning to brighten
NARRATION BEAT: What Wiles had abandoned — Iwasawa theory — is exactly what is missing from the structure he built to replace it. The two approaches, combined, close the gap completely. He will later say it was the most beautiful moment of his life.

---

Scene 8 — What Was Left Behind

The attic room, still. Morning light. The notebook lies open on the desk. Wiles sits without moving. The camera holds in a close shot on his face — not triumphant, not overwhelmed, but quiet in the way that comes after a very long time. Outside the window, an ordinary September morning. The camera slowly pulls back, widening to take in the room: the pinned pages on the walls, the stacked notebooks, the years of work. A man alone in a small room, who found the answer in the thing he had thrown away.

SETTING: Wiles's attic study, Princeton, the morning of September 19, 1994
SUBJECT: Wiles sitting still at his desk; the room behind him
IN-SCENE MOTION: Morning light strengthening through the window; a very slight movement — Wiles exhales
LIGHTING: Brightening morning light, warm and quiet; long shadows from the window frame crossing the floor
NARRATION BEAT: The proof is complete. Fermat's Last Theorem is solved. The tool that turned out to be the missing piece was the one he had set aside years before — judged insufficient, returned to on the last morning before giving up.

---

Key Takeaway

Andrew Wiles spent thirty-two years solving a problem he first saw as a ten-year-old boy — seven of them in secret, one of them in failure. The proof was finally completed with a method he had discarded. He found it on the morning he had set aside to admit defeat.

## Focus Prompt

You are a visionary documentary filmmaker with a strong cinematic voice. This is a human story about obsession, failure, and the unexpected shape of discovery — treat it as a story unfolding over a lifetime, not a lesson in mathematics. Every frame must earn its place. One primary camera action per scene — never combine movements. No text overlays, no captions, no on-screen typography. The tone is quiet and precise: the drama comes from stillness, not spectacle. The camera observes without intruding. Honor the arc: from a boy in a library who makes a private decision, through years of secret work, through failure, to a man sitting alone with an old notebook on the morning he had planned to surrender.
