# Galatians 2: The Truth of the Gospel

E.J. Waggoner's study of Galatians 2:1–10 turns on a single confrontation: men who had received Christ, who had heard the Holy Spirit speak through prophets, who had seen the grace of God at work among them — and were then told it was not enough. The controversy was not peripheral. It was a direct assault on whether Christ alone saves, or whether something more is required.

---

Scene 1 — The Church at Antioch Before the Trouble

In the city of Antioch, a community had been built on remarkable ground. They had come to faith through scattered believers fleeing persecution, been confirmed by Barnabas when he arrived and saw the grace of God at work, and deepened by more than a year of teaching from Paul and Barnabas together. The Holy Spirit had spoken through prophets in their gatherings. They knew what it was to be led by something beyond themselves.

Then certain men arrived from Judea. Their message was simple: "Except ye be circumcised after the manner of Moses, ye cannot be saved."

---

Scene 2 — What That Teaching Actually Said

In the room at Antioch, the message from Judea lands and the faces of the believers change. These are people who have seen the Spirit move, who have heard prophecy, who carry the quiet certainty that something genuinely happened to them. And the men from Judea are telling them: none of it counts. Not yet. Your faith in Christ, the Spirit's witness that you are children of God, the grace at work in your life — it holds nothing until you add the outward sign.

Waggoner puts it plainly: the outward sign without faith was being exalted above faith in Christ without the outward sign. That is not a supplement to the Gospel. It is its opposite. And everyone in that room knew what it meant — everything they had received, the assurance, the peace, the knowledge that something had changed — was being declared insufficient by men who had arrived from Judea with a correction.

---

Scene 3 — The False Brethren

Paul calls these teachers "false brethren" — men who, as the Danish text puts it, had "sneaked in." They had not been sent by the apostles. They were not recognized teachers. They came to spy out, in Paul's words, "our liberty which we have in Christ Jesus, that they might bring us into bondage."

The apostles at Jerusalem later confirmed it: these men had gone out "subverting your souls," and had received no instruction to do so. They were not confused about doctrine. They were, Paul said, to be accursed. The Gospel of Christ was at stake.

---

Scene 4 — The Seal and What It Was For

The outward mark the false brethren were demanding had once meant something. Abraham received it after he had already been declared righteous by faith — it was a seal placed on what had already occurred, a sign of a covenant that existed before the sign was given. It pointed toward the thing itself: trusting God rather than oneself, worshiping in spirit rather than in the performance of outward acts.

What the false brethren were now demanding was the seal without the document. A wax impression stamped on an empty page. These Gentile believers already had what the mark had only ever pointed toward — the inner transformation, the clean heart, the faith of Abraham without Abraham's nationality. They were being told to acquire the symbol of something they already possessed, as though the symbol could authenticate what only God had given.

---

Scene 5 — A Yoke No One Could Bear

At the Jerusalem conference, Peter asked the question that cut to the heart of it: "Why tempt ye God, to put a yoke upon the neck of the disciples, which neither our fathers nor we were able to bear?" The yoke was not the law itself. The law is holy, just, and good. The yoke was being asked to keep the law by their own power, without Christ — to make themselves righteous through self-effort.

Waggoner uses a precise image: the law is a guide-board. It points out the way. It can tell you when you are out of it. But it cannot carry you. Only Christ can do that, because He is the way. Whoever leads people to trust in the law for righteousness, apart from Christ, does not exalt the law — he denies the One in whom alone its righteousness is found.

---

Scene 6 — Paul Goes to Jerusalem by Revelation

Paul went to Jerusalem not to learn the Gospel but to maintain it. He had not been preaching for seventeen years while standing in doubt. He went by revelation — God sent him. And when he met with the pillars of the church, they "added nothing" to him. James, Peter, and John saw the grace given to him and extended the right hand of fellowship.

The conference confirmed what Paul already knew: the Gospel he had received by revelation from Jesus Christ was the same Gospel. He had not needed their endorsement. When God has spoken, Waggoner writes, an endorsement by man is an impertinence.

---

Scene 7 — Open Hands

In that room in Antioch, months before the teachers from Judea arrived, someone had come to faith with nothing in their hands. No credentials. No ancestry. No outward mark. And something had happened — real, interior, witnessed by the Spirit — that no ritual had produced and no effort had maintained.

The lesson Waggoner draws from the whole controversy is this: the Gospel is not a mechanism. It is not activated by an object, unlocked by a ceremony, or completed by a vow. It is the power of God unto salvation — received by faith, held by faith, lived in faith. Paul's words in Ephesians close the argument: "By grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves; it is the gift of God."

Open hands. That is the posture the false brethren were trying to fill with something. And Paul's answer, confirmed in Jerusalem, was that the hands were not empty — they already held everything.

---

Key Takeaway

In a quiet room in Antioch, men who had already received the Spirit were told their faith was not enough — and Paul's answer, confirmed in Jerusalem, was that there is nothing to add. The Gospel is not a starting point that requires human effort to complete. It is the power of God entire, received by faith alone, and anything added to it is not a supplement but a substitution.
