# The Accidental Invention: How a Failed Glue Became the Post-it Note

Spencer Silver was a chemist at 3M in 1968 trying to make a stronger adhesive. He made a weaker one instead. The result sat in the company's files for six years — a solution, as Silver kept saying at internal seminars, in search of a problem. The problem turned out to be a bookmark in a choir hymnal, and the man who had the problem happened to have attended one of Silver's seminars.

---

Scene 1 — The Failed Experiment

St. Paul, Minnesota, 1968. A 3M research laboratory, fluorescent light, rows of benches covered in glassware. Spencer Silver is trying to make a super-strong adhesive for use in aircraft construction. The compound he produces is the opposite: microspheres of acrylic that bond to surfaces but peel off cleanly, leaving no residue, and can be repositioned indefinitely. In any other context this would be a failure. Silver holds up a coated strip of paper, sticks it to the bench, peels it off, sticks it again. The bond is real but it does not hold. He writes up the results carefully and files them. The camera pushes slowly in on the strip of paper on the bench, stuck but not stuck, as the lab activity continues around it.

Scene 2 — The Solution in Search of a Problem

3M headquarters, 1969–1974. A conference room, afternoon. Spencer Silver is presenting his adhesive to a room of colleagues for what is not the first time. He calls it a solution in search of a problem and he means this literally — he believes the compound has a use, he simply has not found it yet. He demonstrates the peel-and-restick. People nod. No one has a use for it. The product does not fit any existing category 3M makes. Silver submits it for a patent and continues giving seminars. The camera tracks slowly along the row of faces watching the demonstration — attentive, slightly puzzled — and stops on one empty chair near the back. Art Fry is not in this room. He will attend a later seminar.

Scene 3 — The Bookmark

Minneapolis, a Sunday morning in 1974. Art Fry is in the choir loft of his church, following the service in a hymnal. A bookmark falls out. He picks it up, repositions it. During the sermon, he thinks about Spencer Silver's adhesive seminar, which he attended months ago and has not thought about since. A bookmark that stays where you put it but comes off cleanly when you're done. The camera holds on the hymnal in his hands, the loose bookmark, the pages — then tilts slowly down to Fry's hands as they go still, the congregation singing around him, the idea arriving.

Scene 4 — The Prototype

3M laboratory, 1974. Fry requests a sample of Silver's adhesive and spends his evenings and weekends in his home basement applying it to small squares of yellow paper — the only paper available in the right weight. He tests them on documents, notebooks, walls. They stay. They come off. They leave nothing behind. He takes samples to the office and begins sticking notes on documents he passes to colleagues. His colleagues begin asking for more. The camera pulls back slowly from a close-up of a yellow square on a white page — Fry's handwriting on it — to reveal the desk, the stack of documents, the basement lab around them.

Scene 5 — The Failed Launch

3M market research, 1977. A test market in four cities — the product is called Write-n-Stick. Sales are flat. Market research shows that consumers asked about a sticky notepad do not understand why they would need one. 3M considers shelving the project. Fry argues that the research method is wrong: you cannot ask people if they want something they have never used. He proposes flooding offices with free samples instead. 3M agrees, reluctantly. In Boise, Idaho, the free sample program runs. Reorder rates come back at 90 percent. The camera moves handheld through a busy office, past desks covered in yellow squares — on monitors, on phones, on files — as if noticing something that was always going to be there.

Scene 6 — The Yellow Square

The present. A desk, any desk, anywhere. A monitor with a yellow square in the corner. A phone with a note stuck to the receiver. A wall with a cluster of them, color-coded. Post-it notes are now made in more than a hundred colors, dozens of sizes, and several dozen countries. 3M sells billions of units a year. The adhesive Spencer Silver invented in 1968 while trying to make something stronger is the only reason any of this exists. The camera rises slowly above the desk to show the office around it — other desks, other yellow squares — until the whole floor is visible and the pattern of small stuck notes maps the entire space.

---

Key Takeaway

Spencer Silver invented the Post-it adhesive by accident in 1968 while trying to make something stronger, spent six years promoting it internally without finding an application, and the application only arrived when Art Fry — who happened to have attended one of Silver's seminars — had a bookmark fall out of a hymnal one Sunday morning. A failed experiment, a forgotten seminar, a bookmark, and a resistant market research process: four near-misses that almost prevented one of the most successful office products ever made.
