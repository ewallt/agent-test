# Paul Cézanne: The Man Who Dismantled the Picture

Paul Cézanne spent most of his career in failure. He submitted to the Paris Salon repeatedly and was rejected almost every time. The Impressionists he exhibited with considered his work unfinished — too crude, the surfaces unresolved, the drawing uncertain. He withdrew to Aix-en-Provence in the south of France and worked in near-total isolation. When he died in 1906, the obituary in the local paper got his name wrong.

Within a decade, Picasso and Braque had used his discoveries to invent Cubism. Matisse bought one of his paintings and kept it for the rest of his life, calling it a "sustaining influence." By 1920 he was widely recognized as the father of modern art. He had been dead for fourteen years.

## The Problem He Was Solving

The Impressionists solved one problem — how to capture light and atmosphere in the moment — and created another. Their paintings shimmered, but they dissolved. Everything became surface, sensation, air. What Cézanne wanted was to "make of Impressionism something solid and durable, like the art of the museums." He wanted the sensation and the structure.

His answer was to stop thinking of a painting as a window onto a scene and start thinking of it as an object in its own right — a flat surface organized into something coherent. Perspective, as it had been practiced since the Renaissance, was a lie: it assumed a single fixed viewpoint, a frozen moment. But looking at anything, you move your eyes. You shift. The mountain doesn't look the same from every position or in every light. Cézanne wanted to put all of that into a single image.

## The Method

Cézanne painted slowly. Agonizingly slowly. Sitters for his portraits reported sitting for over a hundred sessions. His dealer Ambroise Vollard sat for 115 sittings — at the end of which Cézanne declared he was satisfied with the shirt front. He abandoned canvases, left them unfinished, started over. He worked outdoors in the Provençal heat and in his studio, and he painted the same motifs again and again.

His technique involved what critics later called "passage" — a deliberate blurring of the edges between one form and another, so that a tree might bleed into the sky, a rock into a shadow. Contour lines, the clear outlines that academic painters used to define objects, were replaced by small brushstrokes of modulated color. The eye assembles the form rather than being handed it. He also used multiple viewpoints in a single canvas: a bowl of apples might be seen from slightly above while the table beneath it is seen straight on, and the wall behind both is seen from a different angle still. The result is unstable, slightly wrong — and more honest, he believed, to actual experience.

## Mont Sainte-Victoire

The mountain that rises above Aix became Cézanne's obsession and his emblem. He painted it approximately eighty times over twenty-five years — in oil, in watercolor, from different positions, at different hours. The early versions are relatively conventional landscapes. The later ones are barely recognizable as depictions of a mountain at all: planes of color, a structure of interlocking facets, the mountain breaking apart and reassembling.

He was not painting the mountain. He was painting the act of looking at it.

## The Late Recognition

In 1895, the dealer Ambroise Vollard mounted the first major retrospective of Cézanne's work — the artist was 56 years old and had been working in obscurity for decades. The show was a revelation. Young painters came and stared. Pissarro, the Impressionist elder, wept. Monet bought three paintings.

The next generation came to him in pilgrimage. They stood in front of his canvases and understood something that could not quite be articulated — that he had found a way through a door that everyone else was still searching for. He received them cautiously, suspiciously, in his studio in Aix. He did not trust praise; he had been without it for too long.

In October 1906, caught in a rainstorm while painting outdoors, Cézanne collapsed. He was carried home, continued working the next day, and died within the week. He had been working on a painting of Mont Sainte-Victoire.

## What He Left Behind

The flat, fractured surfaces of Cubism. The deliberate distortions of Matisse. The idea that a painting is not a record of a scene but a construction — an object whose relationship to reality is a problem to be solved freshly each time. The entire tradition of twentieth-century painting runs through what Cézanne found in Aix, working alone, rejected, convinced he was failing.

His apples — the bowls of fruit he painted hundreds of times, adjusting coins under the tablecloth to tilt them at the right angle — became the most analyzed still-life objects in the history of art. They look like apples. They also look like the world coming apart and being put back together in a different order.
