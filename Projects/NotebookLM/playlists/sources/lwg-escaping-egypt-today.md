# Escaping Egypt Today

The Exodus is not ancient history. It is the template God uses again and again — and according to the prophets, he intends to use it one final time, on a scale that will make the original look small.

Isaiah announces it with deliberate provocation: "Forget the former things; do not dwell on the past. See, I am doing a new thing!" (Isaiah 43:18–19). The "former thing" is the Exodus from Egypt — the defining miracle of Israel's identity. To say "forget it" is not to minimize it, but to prepare for something greater. The new Exodus will be so overwhelming that the old one will seem, by comparison, a rehearsal.

The prophets developed a rich vocabulary of equivalence: Egypt becomes a symbol for any system that holds people in bondage — political, spiritual, or ideological. When Isaiah describes the return from Babylon using Exodus imagery (dry roads through the sea, water in the desert, the gathering of scattered people), he is saying: the same God who brought Israel out of Egypt is bringing his people out of Babylon. The geography changes; the pattern holds.

Revelation picks up this typology and extends it to its final application. Babylon — the symbolic name for the world system organized around self-salvation, human power, and the rejection of God's authority — is called to account in the same terms as Egypt. The plagues of Revelation echo the plagues of Egypt. The great announcement of Revelation 18:4, "Come out of her, my people," echoes God's command to Israel in the night of the Passover: separate yourselves, mark your households, get ready to leave.

The connection is not incidental. What held Israel in Egypt was not merely Pharaoh's army. It was the system — the whole interlocking economy of empire that made people dependent, productive, and unable to imagine a different life. Babylon in Revelation holds people the same way: "For all the nations have drunk the maddening wine of her adulteries. The kings of the earth committed adultery with her, and the merchants of the earth grew rich from her excessive luxuries" (Revelation 18:3). The bondage is economic, political, spiritual — a total environment.

The call to come out is therefore not primarily geographical. It is a call to disentangle one's life, one's trust, one's identity from a system that promises security and delivers captivity. Egypt offered Israel a living — and took their freedom. Babylon offers the world comfort — and takes its soul.

But the Exodus also carries a promise: the same everlasting covenant that was the foundation of Israel's deliverance is the foundation of the final one. "In a surge of anger I hid my face from you for a moment, but with everlasting kindness I will have compassion on you," says the Lord, your Redeemer. "To me this is like the days of Noah..." (Isaiah 54:8–9). The covenant does not expire. The God who parted the sea will make a way where there is none.

The question the Exodus always poses is: Where is your Egypt? What system, what security, what identity have you built inside walls that were never meant to hold you? The call to come out is as urgent now as the night of the first Passover.
