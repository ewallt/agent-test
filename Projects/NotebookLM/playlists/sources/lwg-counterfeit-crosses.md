# Counterfeit Crosses

There is a cross that saves, and there is a cross that doesn't. The tragedy of religious life is that sincere people can carry the wrong one for a lifetime and never notice.

The counterfeit cross is self-manufactured suffering — the idea that if you hurt enough, deny yourself enough, discipline yourself enough, God will be satisfied. It looks like devotion. It feels like faithfulness. But it is, at its root, an attempt to earn what can only be received.

Jesus said, "Take up your cross and follow me." The key word is *take up* — not *build*. The cross is not something you construct out of your own disciplines and deprivations. It already exists. The only question is whether you will pick it up.

What does the real cross do? It kills self-reliance. Not self — but the self that insists on being its own savior. Paul's language in Galatians is surgical: "I have been crucified with Christ and I no longer live, but Christ lives in me" (Galatians 2:20). The "I" that dies is the performing self, the self that believed its religious effort could close the gap between itself and God.

The counterfeit cross never achieves this. In fact, it has the opposite effect — it inflates the self. Every act of self-denial becomes a credit to one's spiritual account. Every sacrifice becomes a reason for quiet pride. The person carrying a counterfeit cross is not less focused on themselves; they are more focused on themselves, because their suffering has become the currency of their standing before God.

Paul saw this clearly in the Galatian churches. People who had received the gospel of grace were adding requirements — rituals, observances, earned merit — as though Christ's work were incomplete. His response was not gentle: "You foolish Galatians! Who has bewitched you?" (Galatians 3:1). The bewitchment was the appeal to the counterfeit: the idea that human effort could add to what the cross had finished.

The real cross is offensive precisely because it eliminates all boasting. "May I never boast except in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ, through which the world has been crucified to me, and I to the world" (Galatians 6:14). To boast in the cross is to boast in something that required nothing from you — no performance, no qualification, no earned approach. The cross was God's work from start to finish. Our part was to need it.

This does not mean the Christian life is passive. The person who has truly died with Christ is the most active person in the world — but they act from fullness, not from deficit. They are not trying to earn God's favor; they already have it. They are not building toward acceptance; they are living from it. That is the difference between a cross that saves and a cross that merely exhausts.

The question worth sitting with is simple: Is my religious effort a response to love already received, or an attempt to generate love not yet secured? The answer reveals which cross you are carrying.

*Key texts: Matthew 16:24–25; Galatians 2:20; 3:1–3; 6:14; Romans 8:1; Philippians 3:7–9*
