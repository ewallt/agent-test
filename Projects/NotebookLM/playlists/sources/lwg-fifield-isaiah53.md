# Not Smitten of God: The Cross Isaiah Actually Described

## The Line in Isaiah 53

The fifty-third chapter of Isaiah contains a single verse that draws a line between two entirely different views of the cross. The verse is the fourth: "Surely he hath borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows: yet we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted."

George Fifield, preaching at the 1897 General Conference Bible Institute, argued that this verse does not merely describe what happened to Christ. It describes two competing theologies — one true, one false — and names which is which.

The first half of the verse: what was. He bore our griefs. He carried our sorrows. He was pierced through by our misdeeds.

The second half: what we thought was. We esteemed him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted.

Fifield's reading: these are not two descriptions of the same event from different angles. They are a contrast between Christianity and paganism. One is truth. The other, as he puts it plainly, is falsehood.

## What We Got Wrong About the Cross

The pagan conception of sacrifice has a consistent structure across cultures: a god is offended; the god's wrath must be appeased; blood is the price of appeasement. If the offense is ordinary, animal blood will suffice. If the offense is grave, something more costly must flow. When the god smells the blood, the wrath is satisfied, and the worshiper is safe.

Fifield argues that this exact structure was imported wholesale into Christian theology and applied to the cross. The result: God is the one who must be satisfied. Christ's death is the price paid to the Father to appease his wrath. The cross is, on this reading, a transaction in which the Father receives payment before he can relent and forgive.

He quotes the Methodist Discipline of his day as an example: "Christ died to reconcile the Father unto us." Fifield's response: that is paganism, stated plainly. It takes the most extraordinary manifestation of divine love in the history of the universe — the cross — and converts it into a manifestation of wrath being propitiated. What God intended to cause every being in the universe to wonder and adore has been turned into a payment schedule.

## The Reversal Isaiah Actually Describes

The correct reading of every reconciliation and atonement text in Scripture, Fifield argues, is the opposite. Without exception, when Scripture speaks of reconciliation, God is the one doing the reconciling. He is not the one being reconciled. We are.

"God was in Christ, reconciling the world unto himself" (2 Corinthians 5:19). Not reconciling himself to the world. Reconciling the world to himself. The subject and object are fixed throughout the New Testament: God acts; we are acted upon. God gives; we receive. The Father paid — he did not receive payment.

This is Fifield's sharpest formulation: Jesus Christ is not the price paid to the Father for our pardon. He is the price the Father paid to bring us to a repentant attitude of mind, so that he could pardon us freely.

The entire direction of the transaction is reversed. God is not the obstacle to forgiveness. God is the one seeking it. He is the father running down the road before the prodigal's speech is finished. He is the one who says, when I had nothing to pay, he frankly forgave him. The debt in Scripture is always paid by God in Christ — to propitiate us, not himself; to reconcile us, not himself.

## He Bore Our Griefs — Not God's Wrath

Fifield insists on the specificity of Isaiah's words: whose griefs? Whose sorrows? Ours. Not some alien category of divine anger requiring satisfaction. The grief that crushed the heart of Christ and took him from among the living was not something unlike what we bear. It was what we bear.

Christ took our sinful nature, our sinful flesh, at the point of weakness to which the race had brought it. He placed himself where we are, to fight the conflict we have to fight — the fight of faith — using the same power available to us. By the Spirit of God he cast out devils. Through the eternal Spirit he offered himself without spot. The Spirit rested upon him and gave him understanding. He did not fight from a position of invulnerability. He fought as we fight.

This matters for how we understand temptation. Fifield observes that in nine cases out of ten, when people study the temptations in Matthew 4, they make Christ tempted in all points as we are not, rather than as we are. The wilderness temptation follows the exaltation of the baptism — the voice from heaven saying, this is my beloved Son. Then immediately: apparent weakness, hunger, and the devil saying, "If you are the Son of God..." Have we not had this experience? The spiritual high followed by the wilderness. The voice of God followed by the voice of doubt. It was our experience he was living.

He also bore the injustices. Not all suffering is the consequence of personal sin — as long as sin exists, injustice exists, and the innocent suffer for the guilty. God allowed the perfectly innocent one to take the injustice and sin of all, not to appease himself, but because there was healing in it for us.

## The Atonement Is At-One-Ment

What then did the cross accomplish? Fifield reads the atonement as its etymology suggests: at-one-ment. The breaking down of every wall that separates heart from heart — between human and divine, between Jew and Gentile, between the soul and God. He quotes Ephesians 2: we who were far off have been brought near; no longer strangers and foreigners but fellow citizens, built together into a habitation of God through the Spirit.

The goal was not legal clearance. It was proximity. Closeness. God in us and we in God — the prayer of John 17.

The plan of redemption is God's effort to separate sin from the sinner, so that he can destroy the sin and save the sinner. Not destroy the sinner. The wrath of God is against sin; it burns with the same intensity as his love for the sinner, because they are the same thing — the measure of God's hatred for sin is the measure of his love for the person sin is destroying. But the wrath is against the sin, not the person. "As I live, saith the Lord God, I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked." When Christ wept over Jerusalem and pronounced its doom, he was not angry. He said, "How often would I have gathered your children, as a hen gathers her chicks, and you would not." That is how God feels, even when pronouncing judgment: infinite love and only love, from eternity to eternity.

## God Is Not Love Plus Something Else

The standard theological qualification — God is love, but he is also justice — is, Fifield argues, incoherent. The moment you say that, you have introduced a competitor to love inside God, a principle that sometimes constrains or overrides it. You have built a god who must balance love against something else, and you have made justice into something that exists independently of love rather than flowing from it.

Fifield's counter: God is just because he is love. Mercy is the disposition to treat an offender better than he deserves — an attribute of love. Grace is unmerited favor — an attribute of love. Righteousness is love acting out the acts of love. Even the omniscience of God is, in a striking argument, a function of love: only love can comprehend love. Hatred, envy, and jealousy can look infinite Love in the face and not recognize it — they crucified him because they knew him not. God knows all things because he is love, and love comprehends all things.

The wrath of God, too, is an attribute of love. It is not a competing principle but love's response to what threatens its object. The wrath burns against sin to clean the universe — and it will burn until the last trace of sin is gone. It is not satisfied by a payment. It is satisfied by the destruction of sin itself.

## Why the Cross Was Still Necessary

If God's nature is simply to forgive, why was the cross necessary? Fifield's answer is careful. Any pardon that did not deal with sin — that merely cleared a ledger without changing the person — would be imprudent. It would lead more deeply into sin. It would be the indulgent father who gives the child everything the child wants, to the child's destruction.

God is not the obstacle to forgiveness. God wanted to forgive everyone. At the foot of the cross, with sin mocking him in its most unrepentant form, Christ said, "Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do." That is how God felt. He would like to forgive everyone. But a pardon that leads to more sin is not love; it is abandonment. God needed to manifest himself in such a way that we would be won to him — brought to repentance — so that he could pardon us without spoiling us.

That is what the cross is. It is the most costly and complete revelation of God's love, intended to do what nothing else could: show us who God actually is, break through the misunderstanding, and draw us to him in a way that transforms us. Not propitiate God. Propitiate us.

## The Legend of the Holy Grail

Fifield closes with a legend: Launfal sets out to find the Holy Grail, grandly equipped, in the prime of life. A beggar sits at the gate asking alms. Launfal averts his face and flings a coin as he rides past. He travels for years and returns having found nothing. The same beggar is there. But now Launfal is a failure too, and he feels kinship with the man. He puts his hand in his pocket — and this time, his heart goes out with the coin. And the beggar is transformed into the Lord he had been searching for.

The point: Launfal was looking for Christ in the grail, in the grand quest, in the extraordinary. Christ was at the gate. And when Launfal's proud distance collapsed into fellow-feeling, he could finally see him.

God sees not what is, but what is to be. When he loved the cosmos — the word John uses in "God so loved the world" — he was loving order, harmony, beauty, what was possible. He looked at the chaos and loved the cosmos underneath it. The Spirit of God brooding over the chaos of creation, and the Spirit of God brooding over the chaos of a human life, are the same Spirit doing the same thing: seeing the possible, and moving toward it with love.
