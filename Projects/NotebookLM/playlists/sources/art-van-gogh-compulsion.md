# Vincent van Gogh: The Output

Vincent van Gogh did not begin painting seriously until he was 27. He died at 37. In the decade between, he produced roughly 900 paintings and more than 1,100 drawings and sketches. He sold one painting in his lifetime. The buyer was a stranger. The price was 400 francs — about enough to cover his rent for two months. He used it to buy more paint.

## The Letters

Van Gogh wrote constantly. More than 800 letters survive, most of them to his brother Theo, who was an art dealer in Paris and who supported Vincent financially for most of his adult life. The letters are not the correspondence of a tortured man crying for help. They are the working notes of a serious thinker — dense with observations about technique, about color theory, about Rembrandt and Delacroix and Millet, about what he was trying to do and why it wasn't working yet. "I feel a power in me that I must develop," he wrote in 1880, "a fire that I may not quench." He was 27, had tried and failed at art dealing, teaching, and ministry, and had just decided to become a painter.

## The Dark Period and the Turn to Color

His early work is almost entirely dark — Dutch peasants, miners, weavers, the laboring poor. The Potato Eaters (1885), his first major work, is painted in near-black tones, the figures rough and unglamorous. He wanted to show what it actually looked like to earn your food from the earth. Academic painters prettified poverty. He refused to.

Then he moved to Paris in 1886 and encountered the Impressionists. The effect was immediate and total. His palette exploded. He started using complementary colors — orange against blue, yellow against violet — at a saturation that no Impressionist had dared. Where Monet dissolved form into light, Van Gogh hardened it. His brushstrokes became thick, directional, loaded. The paint itself became a physical record of intensity.

## Arles

In 1888 he moved south to Arles, seeking the light and color of Provence. He rented a yellow house and worked at a pace that staggers the imagination: in the fifteen months he spent in Arles and the nearby Saint-Paul-de-Mausole asylum, he produced approximately 300 paintings. That is roughly one every day and a half.

He invited Paul Gauguin to come and paint with him, dreaming of a community of artists. Gauguin arrived in October 1888. The visit lasted nine weeks. They argued constantly about art, about technique, about everything. On the night of December 23rd, Van Gogh cut off part of his own ear and brought it to a woman at a local brothel. Gauguin left the next morning and never returned.

The breakdown did not stop the work. From the asylum at Saint-Paul, Van Gogh continued painting — the garden, the wheat fields visible from his window, the night sky. The Starry Night was painted there. The olive groves series. The irises. He was allowed outside to paint under supervision, and he used every minute.

## The Final Seventy Days

In May 1890 he moved to Auvers-sur-Oise, a village north of Paris, to be closer to Theo. Dr. Paul Gachet, an amateur painter and physician, agreed to look after him. In the seventy days between his arrival and his death, Van Gogh painted seventy-six canvases. One painting per day.

On July 27th, 1890, he walked into a wheat field outside the village and shot himself in the chest. He walked back to the inn, climbed to his room, and lay down. He died two days later, on July 29th, with Theo beside him. He was 37. Theo died six months later, broken, some said, by grief.

## What He Was After

Van Gogh's letters describe a painter who understood exactly what he was trying to do. He wanted to use color "to express the terrible passions of humanity" — not to describe a scene but to create an experience. He believed color could carry emotion directly, without the mediation of story or symbol. The yellow of the sunflowers is not a description of yellow; it is yellow used to produce a state of feeling in the person standing in front of it.

He was also trying to make art for ordinary people. The peasants and working men he had lived among in the Netherlands. Not paintings for collectors or academies — paintings that someone who worked with their hands could look at and feel something true.

He never saw whether it worked. The audience came after he was gone. Today The Starry Night hangs in the Museum of Modern Art in New York and is one of the most recognized images in the world. The wheat field outside Auvers is still there. There is a small stone marker where he fell.
