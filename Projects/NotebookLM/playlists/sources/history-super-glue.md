# The Accident in the Parts Box: How Super Glue Was Invented Twice

Harry Coover was a chemist at Eastman Kodak in 1942 trying to make clear plastic gun sights for Allied weapons. He produced a compound called cyanoacrylate that bonded instantly to everything it touched and could not be separated. It was useless for gun sights. Coover noted the results and moved on. Nine years later, one of his colleagues accidentally glued two prisms together with the same compound, and Coover recognized it immediately. The same useless adhesive. He had invented Super Glue twice, and the first time he had thrown it away.

---

Scene 1 — The War Contract

An Eastman Kodak laboratory, Rochester, New York, 1942. The war is two years old and the military wants clearer, lighter gun sight lenses. Harry Coover and his team are working through candidate compounds systematically — synthesize, test, discard. Cyanoacrylate bonds instantly to everything: the test surfaces, the instruments, the glass slides, his fingers. There is no separation method. It cannot be used for precision optics. Coover records the results in his lab notebook with the same notational economy he applies to everything else. The camera pushes slowly in on the notebook entry — a single line describing the compound's properties — as the lab activity continues around it, none of it suggesting that the line matters.

Scene 2 — Filed Away

A Kodak research archive, 1942–1951. Nine years of other projects. Coover moves on to other assignments; the cyanoacrylate notes go into the files. The camera tracks slowly along rows of shelved notebooks, identical in format, dozens of them, pausing briefly on the spine of one among hundreds. Nothing marks it as different from any other. The research that will become the best-selling adhesive in history sits in a grey binder on a shelf while the postwar world builds around it.

Scene 3 — The Prisms

A Kodak laboratory, 1951. A researcher named Fred Joyner is testing new compounds for a heat-resistant jet canopy coating. He applies cyanoacrylate between two optical prisms to test the refraction properties. When he tries to separate them, they will not move. The equipment is bonded permanently. Joyner looks at the compound, then at the prisms, then back at the compound. The camera holds on his face — not alarm, something closer to recognition of the wrong thing happening — and then cuts to the bonded prisms on the bench, two pieces of glass fused as if they had always been one object.

Scene 4 — Coover Recognizes It

A Kodak office, 1951. Joyner reports the problem to Coover, who reads the compound designation and goes very still. He knows this one. He synthesized it himself nine years ago in a different laboratory under a different contract. Coover understands immediately what Joyner has rediscovered: not an error, a property — the same property he had discarded as useless in 1942 is now, in a different context, exactly what is needed. The camera pulls back slowly from Coover at his desk to reveal the full office around him — the filing cabinets, the archived notebooks — the institutional memory that almost lost the compound for good.

Scene 5 — Eastman 910

Kodak laboratories and marketing offices, 1958. Coover and his team develop a commercial formulation and Kodak launches it under the name Eastman 910. Coover demonstrates it on television: a single drop bonds a steel cable strong enough to lift the host's entire body weight off the ground. The audience reacts the way audiences react to things that contradict their model of how materials behave. The camera follows the steel cable from the bonded joint upward to the suspended host — the single drop of adhesive holding everything — then tilts slowly down to Coover's hands, the same hands that had handled the compound in 1942 and set it aside.

Scene 6 — The Parts Box

A hardware store, any year after 1958. A wall of adhesives, dozens of brands. Super Glue — the category, the generic noun, the cultural shorthand for permanent bonding — occupies its section of the shelf. The compound Coover discarded in 1942 as useless for gun sights is now used in surgery, forensics, dentistry, and kitchen drawers in a hundred countries. The camera moves slowly along the shelf, past the different brands and formulations, and settles on a single tube — the kind of object that no one looks at twice — as if it were the most ordinary thing in the world, which by now it is.

---

Key Takeaway

Harry Coover synthesized cyanoacrylate in 1942, found it useless for its intended purpose, and filed it away. Nine years later a colleague accidentally bonded two prisms together with the same compound. Coover recognized it immediately — he had been the one who discarded it. The adhesive had not changed. The context had. Super Glue was not invented once by accident; it was invented once by accident, set aside, and then rediscovered by a different accident nine years later.
