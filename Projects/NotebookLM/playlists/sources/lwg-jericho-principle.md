# The Jericho Principle

No swords were drawn at Jericho. No siege engines were built. No assault was planned. Israel marched around the city once a day for six days in silence — and on the seventh day, they shouted, and the walls fell.

The strategy was not military. It was theological. God had already given them the city (Joshua 6:2 — past tense: "I *have* delivered Jericho into your hands"). The march was not the means of victory. It was the public expression of belief in a victory already accomplished.

This is the Jericho Principle: the decisive battle is won before the visible struggle begins. The people's job is not to generate the outcome but to act in accordance with a promise already made.

The principle runs throughout Scripture's account of how God works at critical moments in history. Gideon's three hundred against a numberless army — torches and jars, not swords. David against Goliath — a shepherd with a sling, unarmored, running toward the giant. Jehoshaphat sending the choir ahead of the army (2 Chronicles 20:21–22). In each case, the conventional calculus of power is inverted. The victory belongs to the one who has no earthly reason to be confident — except a word from God.

The book of Revelation describes a final conflict using the same logic. The victory of God's people is not won by political maneuvering, by accumulating earthly power, by building coalitions with the institutions of the present age. The great announcement of Revelation 18 — "Fallen! Fallen is Babylon the Great!" — is spoken in the past tense, from heaven, before the visible event has unfolded on earth. The heavenly reality precedes the earthly outcome. God's people are called to align their lives with that heavenly reality and come out of Babylon — not to defeat it by force, but to separate from it by faith.

Zechariah gave the covenant people the summary principle centuries before Jericho was a memory: "'Not by might nor by power, but by my Spirit,' says the Lord Almighty" (Zechariah 4:6). The Spirit is the effective agent. Human might and power are not the currency in which God's kingdom transacts.

This is deeply counterintuitive for people formed by a culture of effectiveness, strategy, and measurable outcomes. The church has often responded to hostility with the tools of political power — lobbying, institutional pressure, the machinery of earthly influence — as though the right strategy could secure what only the Spirit can accomplish.

The Jericho Principle is not passivity. Israel still had to march. They had to get up every morning for seven days and do something that, from a military standpoint, made no sense. The march was costly — it required the discipline of silence, the exposure of vulnerability, the willingness to look foolish. Faith is never passive. But faith directs its energy toward obedience, not toward generating the outcome on its own terms.

The walls of Jericho fell because God said they would, and because a people believed him enough to march. That is still the shape of how God's decisive work happens.
