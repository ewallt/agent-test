**1. Slide 1: The Famishing Flock**
*   **Title:** "As Dry as the Hills of Gilboa"
*   **Paragraph A:** In the late 1880s, the Seventh-day Adventist Church had labored faithfully to establish its distinctive doctrines — the seventh-day Sabbath, the state of the dead, the prophecies of Daniel and Revelation. Its ministers were earnest and its arguments were sound. But something essential had gone missing from the pulpit. The doctrines were being preached, but the Savior was not.
*   **Paragraph B:** Ellen G. White named the crisis with characteristic bluntness. "As a people we have preached the law until we are as dry as the hills of Gilboa, that had neither dew nor rain," she wrote in the Review and Herald, March 1890. The image was deliberate — Gilboa, where Saul died and David cursed the ground with desolation, was a landscape of spiritual withdrawal. The congregation was famishing, and the preachers, so certain of their doctrine, could not see why.

**2. Slide 2: Minneapolis, October 1888**
*   **Title:** The Conference That Changed Everything
*   **Paragraph A:** In October 1888, the General Conference of Seventh-day Adventists convened in Minneapolis, Minnesota. The formal agenda included a doctrinal question about the meaning of "the law" in the book of Galatians. But beneath that surface question lay a far deeper inquiry: what was the true relationship between law and grace, between human effort and divine mercy, between commandment-keeping and living faith in Jesus Christ?
*   **Paragraph B:** George Butler, president of the General Conference, had prepared a thorough scholarly pamphlet laying out the established interpretation. Into this setting came two younger voices with a different answer — E. J. Waggoner and A. T. Jones, who had been studying the New Testament epistles with fresh attention to the theme of righteousness by faith. And behind them, lending weight to the proceedings by her presence and watchful interest, was the church's most trusted prophetic voice, Ellen G. White.

**3. Slide 3: Two Pamphlets, Two Visions**
*   **Title:** "The Law in Galatians" vs. "The Gospel in Galatians"
*   **Paragraph A:** The contrast between the two approaches could be read in the titles of their pamphlets alone. Butler's framing — "The Law in Galatians" — positioned the question as a matter of legal interpretation: which law did Paul mean? Ceremonial or moral? The argument was conducted on the terrain of textual scholarship and ecclesiastical precedent. Ellet J. Waggoner's framing reoriented the entire question. "The Gospel in Galatians" declared that the issue in Galatia was not which law, but what any law was pointing toward.
*   **Paragraph B:** Waggoner, a physician-theologian of uncommon depth, and Alonzo T. Jones, a passionate and unconventional preacher, had come to Minneapolis carrying a message about righteousness by faith — that the righteousness required before God was not human achievement but Christ's own perfect record, received by faith and credited to the sinner. Their central claim was not a departure from Adventist conviction but a deeper penetration into it: the law and the Savior could not be rightly understood apart from each other. Minneapolis was the moment that claim would be fully and publicly heard.

**4. Slide 4: What Was the Real Issue?**
*   **Title:** "No — It Was Righteousness by Faith"
*   **Paragraph A:** A young Adventist minister named Washburn came to Ellen White during the Minneapolis meetings, genuinely puzzled about what the central issue before the conference actually was. He had followed the discussions carefully and believed he understood the question at stake. He sought her out to clarify his thinking — and found instead that his entire framing of the issue needed to be fundamentally reoriented.
*   **Paragraph B:** White asked him directly: "Do you know what the issue was of contention?" He replied with confidence: "Of course — the law in Galatians." White shook her head. "No," she said. "It was righteousness by faith." The exchange cuts to the heart of Minneapolis. The surface debate about Galatian law was real enough — but the deeper question was whether the Adventist church would preach its doctrines as an end in themselves, or preach them as a revelation of Christ and His saving grace. Washburn had the right facts and the wrong question.

**5. Slide 5: "Every Fiber of My Heart Said Amen"**
*   **Title:** The Moment of Recognition
*   **Paragraph A:** When Waggoner stood to present his message, Ellen White was hearing it for the first time. She had not coordinated with him beforehand — she had simply ensured that his voice would be heard in the proceedings alongside the other presentations. She listened as Waggoner set forth the righteousness of Christ as the sinner's only standing before God, and as he showed how justification by faith, far from undermining the law, brought the believer into genuine harmony with it. Later she wrote that "every fiber of my heart said, 'Amen.'"
*   **Paragraph B:** What moved her was not novelty but recognition — the sense that something essential, long obscured, had been restored to its rightful place. The message of righteousness by faith was not a departure from Adventist theology but its completion. The grace that forgave the sinner was the same grace that began writing the law upon the heart. This was what had been missing from the dry hills of Gilboa. This was the dew and rain the famishing flock needed. The 1888 message had named the five pillars of the preaching that would supply it.

**6. Slide 6: Pillar One — The Bible as Ultimate Authority**
*   **Title:** "Let the Word of God Speak to the People"
*   **Paragraph A:** The foundation beneath every other pillar of powerful preaching is Scripture itself as the ultimate and exclusive authority of the sermon. Ellen White's diagnosis of the spiritual drought she named at Gilboa traced back, at its root, to a displacement of the Word. When theological argument had become the preacher's primary instrument — building cases, establishing doctrinal positions through chains of human reasoning — the voice of God in Scripture had been quietly crowded to the margins. The preacher's own logic was speaking where the Word alone had the right to speak.
*   **Paragraph B:** White's corrective was unambiguous: "Ministers must proclaim the Word of God rather than human traditions, opinions, or fables." A sermon built on the authority of Scripture is not merely more persuasive — it is categorically different in kind. It carries with it the life of the Word itself, not merely the force of an argument. Every other pillar of powerful preaching stands on this one. Christ can only be truly set forth if Scripture sets Him forth. The law and the gospel can only be rightly understood through the lens of divine revelation. The preacher who is anchored in the Word is anchored in the only source that can feed the famishing flock.

**7. Slide 7: Pillar Two — Christ the Center**
*   **Title:** "Put Christ Into Every Sermon"
*   **Paragraph A:** The second pillar is the one most directly addressed by the 1888 message, and the most urgent in Ellen White's homiletical writings: Jesus Christ must be the living center of every sermon. Not mentioned in passing, not assumed in the background, not arrived at after the doctrinal argument has been made — named, exalted, and dwelt upon from the beginning. "There have been entire discourses, dry and Christless, in which Jesus has scarcely been named," White lamented. This was the desolation she had compared to Gilboa.
*   **Paragraph B:** Her command was precise: "Put Christ into every sermon. Let the preciousness, mercy, and glory of Jesus Christ be dwelt upon until Christ is formed within, the hope of glory." The 1888 message was compelling not simply because it was theologically correct, but because it placed Christ — His person, His righteousness, His sacrifice, His mediatorial work in the heavenly sanctuary — at the center of every argument. The flock was not famishing for more doctrine. It was famishing for more of the Savior. These are not the same hunger, and a faithful preacher must know the difference.

**8. Slide 8: Pillar Three — The Earnest Preacher**
*   **Title:** The Preacher Who Has Been Changed
*   **Paragraph A:** The third pillar addresses not the content of a sermon but its source. Ellen White drew a sharp distinction between preaching designed to win a debate and preaching that flows from a life personally changed by the grace it announces. The flock can sense the difference. Correct doctrine, delivered by a preacher who has not himself been broken and remade by the love of God, leaves the hearers unmoved — not because the doctrine is wrong, but because it has not yet passed through the crucible of personal experience. The congregation receives information where it needed testimony.
*   **Paragraph B:** "The minister's utterances, to be effectual, must reach the hearts of his hearers," White taught. Her challenge to every preacher was direct and searching: "Dare not to preach another discourse until you know, by your own experience, what Christ is to you." A preacher whose heart has been melted by divine love will melt hearts in return — not through oratorical display or clever argument, but through the irresistible witness of someone who has personally encountered the grace he proclaims. Earnestness is not a style. It is the overflow of a heart that has been genuinely changed.

**9. Slide 9: Pillar Four — Law Blended with the Gospel**
*   **Title:** The Mirror and the Remedy, Together
*   **Paragraph A:** The fourth pillar is the blending of law and gospel into a single, unified message. These two are not theological opponents to be assigned to separate sermons — they are partners in the one work of salvation, moving in two essential and inseparable directions. In the first movement, the law functions as a mirror, showing the sinner the precise shape of their need — not to condemn and leave them there, but to create an acute awareness that drives them to Christ, the only source of remedy. A sermon that bypasses the law has removed the very mechanism that makes the gospel felt.
*   **Paragraph B:** In the second movement, the gospel does what the law cannot: it transforms. The new covenant promise — "I will put my law in their minds and write it on their hearts" (Jeremiah 31:33) — is not a distant aspiration but the present fruit of genuine faith in Christ. This was the specific insight that had thrilled White at Minneapolis: that justification by faith brings the believer into genuine harmony with the law, not away from it. Grace and obedience are not in tension. They are the same movement of God, blended in the preacher's message as they are blended in the gospel itself.

**10. Slide 10: Pillar Five — The Sure Word of Prophecy**
*   **Title:** Sap and Nourishment for the Famishing Flock
*   **Paragraph A:** The fifth pillar is the sure word of prophecy. Ellen White taught that powerful preaching must include the great prophetic themes of Daniel and Revelation — Christ as High Priest ministering in the heavenly sanctuary, interceding for His people in the judgment hour, and the certainty of His imminent return. These are not optional additions to the gospel message but its cosmic framework. The plan of salvation unfolds across the sweep of prophetic history, and a congregation that grasps prophecy understands why the gospel is not merely beautiful but urgent.
*   **Paragraph B:** Together the five pillars define the preaching that can restore the hills of Gilboa. "We must preach Christ in the law," White wrote, "and there will be sap and nourishment in the preaching that will be as food to the famishing flock of God." The five pillars — Scripture as ultimate authority, Christ at the center, the earnest preacher who has been changed, law blended with gospel, the sure prophetic word — describe not a homiletical technique but a person. The preacher who embodies them cannot speak of doctrine without speaking of the One the doctrine reveals. This is what the famishing flock has always needed. It needs it still.
