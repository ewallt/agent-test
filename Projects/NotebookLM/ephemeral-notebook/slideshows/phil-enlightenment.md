**1. Slide 1: Three Minds That Broke Philosophy Open**
*   **Title:** How Three Philosophers Changed What We Can Know
*   **Paragraph A:** In the span of roughly 150 years, three thinkers — René Descartes, David Hume, and Immanuel Kant — dismantled and rebuilt the foundations of human knowledge. Each one inherited the wreckage left by the previous, and each one left the problem in a more difficult and more honest state than he found it. Together, they define what is now called the Enlightenment revolution in philosophy.
*   **Paragraph B:** The central question running through all three was deceptively simple: what can we know, and how do we know it? Descartes thought certainty was possible if we reasoned carefully enough. Hume showed why Descartes was wrong. Kant showed why Hume's conclusion, though devastating, need not be the end of the story.

**2. Slide 2: Descartes Tears Everything Down**
*   **Title:** What If Everything You Believe Is Wrong?
*   **Paragraph A:** René Descartes, writing in Paris in 1641, set himself an extraordinary task: find a foundation for knowledge so solid that it could not be doubted. His method was to doubt everything first. He imagined an all-powerful evil demon deceiving him at every moment — planting false sensory impressions, distorting his memories, even making him believe in a physical world that did not exist. Could he trust his senses? No. Could he trust his memories? No. Could he even trust mathematics?
*   **Paragraph B:** This was not nihilism but a deliberate surgical procedure. Descartes was stripping away every belief that could conceivably be false, looking for anything that survived. If something could survive the assault of total doubt, it would be genuinely indubitable — worth building on. Most of what we take for granted did not survive the test.

**3. Slide 3: One Thing Survives**
*   **Title:** I Think, Therefore I Am
*   **Paragraph A:** After destroying everything he believed, Descartes found one claim that could not be doubted: the very act of doubting proved he existed. "I have convinced myself that there is absolutely nothing in the world, no sky, no earth, no minds, no bodies. Doesn't it follow that I don't exist? No, surely I must exist if it's me who is convinced of something." Even a deceiving demon required something to deceive — a thinker. Cogito, ergo sum.
*   **Paragraph B:** From this single certainty, Descartes defined himself as a "thinking thing" — res cogitans — something that doubts, understands, affirms, denies, wills, and imagines. He then used a proof of God's existence to restore trust in his clear and distinct perceptions of the external world. Mind and body, he argued, were fundamentally different substances — a dualism that would generate philosophical problems for centuries to come.

**4. Slide 4: Hume Starts Over**
*   **Title:** All Knowledge Begins With Experience
*   **Paragraph A:** David Hume, writing in Edinburgh in 1739, approached the problem of knowledge from the opposite direction. Where Descartes began with pure reason and used it to rescue the external world, Hume began with the external world and used it to cut reason down to size. For Hume, every idea in the mind is ultimately traceable to a sensory impression — something directly experienced. Abstract reasoning disconnected from experience produces nothing reliable about reality.
*   **Paragraph B:** Hume divided all knowledge into two kinds: relations of ideas (truths that are necessarily true by definition, like mathematics) and matters of fact (truths about the world, established only through experience). Crucially, no matter of fact can be proven by reason alone. And his most startling conclusion: reason is utterly powerless to motivate action or ground morality. "Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions."

**5. Slide 5: Causation Is a Habit, Not a Law**
*   **Title:** We Never See Cause — Only Sequence
*   **Paragraph A:** Hume's most devastating argument concerned causation — the bedrock of all scientific reasoning. When one billiard ball strikes another and the second moves, we feel certain the first caused the second to move. But what exactly did we observe? One event, then another. We never saw any necessary connection between them — only constant conjunction, one event reliably following another. The idea that one must cause the other is a habit of mind, not a fact about the world.
*   **Paragraph B:** Repeated experience of event A followed by event B produces an expectation that B will follow A in the future. But there is no logical or rational basis for that expectation. The future is not guaranteed to resemble the past. This is the Problem of Induction: all scientific laws, all predictions, all practical reasoning rest on an assumption that cannot itself be rationally grounded. Hume did not claim induction was useless — only that it was a habit, not a proof.

**6. Slide 6: The Self Is a Story We Tell**
*   **Title:** There Is No Persistent Self — Only Perceptions
*   **Paragraph A:** Hume extended his empiricism inward, with results as unsettling as his attack on causation. Descartes had placed a unified, enduring soul at the center of his philosophy — the thinking thing that persisted through time and gave identity to the self. Hume looked inward and found no such thing. "For my part, when I enter most intimately into what I call myself, I always stumble on some particular perception or other... I never can catch myself at any time without a perception."
*   **Paragraph B:** There is no persistent self behind the perceptions — just a stream of fleeting impressions in constant flux. The "self" is a bundle of perceptions, a convenient fiction the mind constructs to make sense of its own experience. Hume applied the same skepticism to religion, arguing that it is always more probable that witness testimony is false than that a law of nature was violated — a direct challenge to the foundations of revealed religion.

**7. Slide 7: Kant Is Woken Up**
*   **Title:** The Problem That Interrupted His Dogmatic Slumber
*   **Paragraph A:** Immanuel Kant, professor of philosophy in Königsberg, read Hume and was shaken. "I freely admit that it was the remembrance of David Hume which, many years ago, first interrupted my dogmatic slumber and gave my investigations in the field of speculative philosophy a completely different direction." Hume had shown that reason could not ground causation, induction, or the self — yet Kant believed that mathematics and Newtonian physics were genuinely certain. Something had to give.
*   **Paragraph B:** Kant spent more than a decade working out his response. The result, published in 1781 as the Critique of Pure Reason, was one of the most ambitious and difficult works in the history of philosophy. Its central claim was a complete reversal of the assumptions both Descartes and Hume had shared: that the mind passively receives and reflects an independent reality. Kant argued the opposite was true.

**8. Slide 8: The Mind Shapes What It Knows**
*   **Title:** Objects Conform to the Mind — Not the Other Way Around
*   **Paragraph A:** Kant's Copernican revolution in philosophy: rather than the mind conforming to objects, objects conform to the structures of the mind. The mind does not passively receive reality — it actively organizes sensory data by imposing its own forms of intuition (space and time) and categories of understanding (causality, substance, and others) onto raw experience. This is why mathematics and physics are necessarily true: they describe the structures the mind itself provides, not contingent facts about an independent world. "Thoughts without contents are empty, perceptions without conceptions are blind."
*   **Paragraph B:** The consequence is a sharp boundary around human knowledge. We can only ever know the world as it appears to us — phenomena — filtered through the mind's innate structures. We can never know reality as it is in itself, independent of all perception — the noumenal world, the thing-in-itself. Traditional metaphysics — God, the soul, free will — lies beyond the boundary of what theoretical reason can reach. This was not defeat but clarification: the proper limits of human knowledge finally drawn.

**9. Slide 9: Dare to Know**
*   **Title:** Sapere Aude — Have Courage to Use Your Own Understanding
*   **Paragraph A:** In 1784, Kant published a short essay answering the question "What is Enlightenment?" His answer has never been surpassed: "Enlightenment is man's emergence from his self-imposed immaturity. Immaturity is the inability to use one's understanding without guidance from another. Sapere Aude! Have courage to use your own understanding! — that is the motto of enlightenment." Most people remain intellectually immature, he argued, not from lack of intelligence but from lack of courage — it is easier and more comfortable to let others think for them.
*   **Paragraph B:** Kant distinguished between the private use of reason (which institutions can legitimately constrain) and the public use of reason (which must be completely free). His own age, he acknowledged, was not yet enlightened — but it was an age of enlightenment, moving in the right direction. The motto Sapere Aude remains the most concise statement of what the Enlightenment project was ultimately asking of every human being.

**10. Slide 10: What the Revolution Achieved**
*   **Title:** From Certainty to Skepticism to Synthesis
*   **Paragraph A:** The arc from Descartes to Hume to Kant is the arc of philosophy learning to be honest about its own limits. Descartes sought perfect certainty and thought he found it in the cogito. Hume dismantled certainty about causation, induction, the self, and God — leaving only experience and habit. Kant accepted Hume's critique and built something new from it: a philosophy that rescued scientific knowledge without pretending to know more than we can.
*   **Paragraph B:** What the three thinkers share is the conviction that the question of knowledge matters — that how we justify our beliefs, what counts as evidence, and where reason reaches its limits are not academic puzzles but the foundation on which everything else rests. The Enlightenment did not give us final answers. It gave us harder, more honest questions — and the courage, as Kant would say, to face them without flinching.
