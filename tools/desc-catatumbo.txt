Where the Catatumbo River meets Lake Maracaibo in northwestern Venezuela, a lightning storm appears almost every night of the year — roughly 260 nights, up to ten hours at a time, as many as 40,000 strikes in a single night. Spanish sailors in the 16th century called it the Lighthouse of Maracaibo. They could see it 40 kilometers out at sea and used it to navigate into the harbor. It is the most lightning-dense place on earth.

The storm is made entirely of geography. Warm, moisture-laden air from the lake collides each evening with cold air flowing down from the Andes; the terrain funnels this collision to the same location every night. Local fishermen go out after dark and use the lightning as ambient light. In January 2010 a regional drought stopped the storm for six weeks — and people across Venezuela described it as something they could feel, an absence in the sky. When the rains returned, the lightning returned within days: 40,000 strikes per night, exactly where it had always been.
