The Dead Sea sits 430 meters below sea level — the lowest point on the surface of the earth. Its water is ten times saltier than the ocean. A person floats in it without trying, lying on their back reading a newspaper. Resorts were built at its edge. Tourists came. The Dead Sea was a wonder, a destination, a curiosity. It has been quietly disappearing for sixty years.

The water level drops more than a meter every year. The Jordan River — the sea's only significant inflow — once delivered 1.3 billion cubic meters annually; today it delivers less than 3%, diverted for agriculture. Shorelines have retreated hundreds of meters from docks that once stood at the water's edge. As the fresh water table drops, it dissolves ancient underground salt deposits, and the earth above collapses without warning. There are now more than 6,000 sinkholes in the surrounding land. The old water marks are still visible on the cliffs. They are not geological time. They are living memory.
