In 1975, Nauru was among the richest countries on earth per capita. A coral atoll in the central Pacific — twenty-one square kilometers, eight miles around — its interior turned out to be solid phosphate rock: millions of years of seabird guano compressed into the fertilizer the world needed. No income tax. Free health care. Free education. Free airfare. The island stripped itself bare to pay for all of it.

By 2001 the phosphate was nearly gone, and so was the money. The trust fund had been invested in bad bonds and a failed West End musical. More than eighty percent of the island's interior was now the Topside — a field of limestone pinnacles with nothing between them, nothing growing, nothing coming back in any human lifetime. When the money ran out, Nauru leased itself to Australia as an offshore detention facility for asylum seekers. The island had become a resource of a different kind.
