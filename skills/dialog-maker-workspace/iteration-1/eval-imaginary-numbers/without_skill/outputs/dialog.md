# Why Are Imaginary Numbers Called "Imaginary"?

*A professor-student dialog*

---

**Student:** Professor, I've been working through complex numbers and something keeps bothering me. Why are they called "imaginary"? It sounds like mathematicians are admitting they made something up.

**Professor:** That's a sharp observation, and honestly, you've put your finger on one of the great naming regrets in the history of mathematics. Descartes coined the term "imaginary" in 1637 — and he meant it as a dismissal. He was essentially saying: these numbers don't really exist, they're just a fiction we're forced to write down.

**Student:** So it was an insult?

**Professor:** Precisely. The square root of negative one showed up in formulas — Cardano's work on cubic equations, for instance — and mathematicians found it deeply unsettling. You square any real number, positive or negative, and you get something positive. So what kind of number, when squared, gives you a negative? It seemed impossible. Imaginary was a polite way of saying "nonsense."

**Student:** And the name just... stuck?

**Professor:** It did. Leibniz called them "amphibians between being and non-being." Euler, who did more than almost anyone to make them respectable, still used the word imaginary. By the time people understood what these numbers really were, the name had calcified. Gauss lobbied hard to replace "imaginary" with "lateral" — which is actually a much more descriptive term, as we'll see — but he lost the battle.

**Student:** Okay, so what are they actually? If they're not imaginary in the sense of fake, what's the real story?

**Professor:** Let's build up to it. You know the real number line — every real number sits at some point on a line stretching from negative infinity to positive infinity. Now, think about what multiplication does geometrically. When you multiply a number by negative one, you rotate it 180 degrees around the origin. Positive three becomes negative three. You've flipped it to the other side.

**Student:** I've never thought of multiplication as rotation before.

**Professor:** Most people haven't. But watch what happens. If multiplying by negative one is a 180-degree rotation, what operation would represent a 90-degree rotation? You'd need something where doing it twice gives you a 180-degree rotation — in other words, something where doing it twice is the same as multiplying by negative one.

**Student:** Oh. So you need a number where... squaring it gives negative one.

**Professor:** Exactly. That's i. The imaginary unit isn't some mystical fiction — it's the 90-degree rotation operator. When you multiply a real number by i, you rotate it 90 degrees counterclockwise on a plane.

**Student:** Wait — a plane? That's where complex numbers come in?

**Professor:** Yes. The real numbers live on a line. But once you accept i, you need a second dimension to put it. The complex plane — also called the Argand plane — has the real numbers on the horizontal axis and the imaginary numbers on the vertical axis. A complex number like 3 + 2i is just a point: three units right, two units up.

**Student:** So Gauss wanted to call them "lateral" because they're on the lateral axis — the vertical one?

**Professor:** Precisely. "Lateral numbers" would have been far less confusing to generations of students. But here we are.

**Student:** This rotation thing — can you show me how it works concretely?

**Professor:** Gladly. Take the number 1, which sits at the point (1, 0) on the complex plane. Now multiply it by i. You get i, which sits at (0, 1) — you've rotated 90 degrees counterclockwise. Multiply by i again: i times i is i-squared, which equals negative one, sitting at (-1, 0). That's 180 degrees total. Multiply by i once more: you get negative i, at (0, -1) — 270 degrees. And one more time brings you back to 1 at (1, 0). A full 360-degree rotation.

**Student:** That's elegant. So i isn't weird at all — it's just the geometry of rotation encoded as arithmetic.

**Professor:** That's a beautiful way to put it. And this is why complex numbers are so powerful in physics and engineering. Any time you're dealing with rotation, oscillation, or waves — anything that cycles — complex numbers appear naturally. Electrical engineers use them constantly for AC circuits. Quantum mechanics is written in the language of complex numbers. Signal processing, fluid dynamics, control theory — they're everywhere.

**Student:** Because the real world has rotation in it.

**Professor:** And oscillation, which is rotation projected onto a line. Think of a weight on a spring. It bounces up and down in a pattern that, if you could see it from the right angle, is actually a point moving in a circle. The sine and cosine functions that describe that motion are secretly the real and imaginary parts of a rotating complex number. That's Euler's formula: e to the power of i-theta equals cosine-theta plus i-sine-theta.

**Student:** So Euler's formula isn't a coincidence — it's describing literal rotation.

**Professor:** Yes. e to the i-pi equals negative one — the most famous equation in mathematics — is simply saying: if you rotate 180 degrees, you end up at negative one. It's almost mundane once you see it geometrically. Almost.

**Student:** I want to go back to something. You said these numbers showed up in Cardano's cubic formula. Why? What do roots of negative numbers have to do with solving cubic equations?

**Professor:** This is where the history gets delicious. When you solve a cubic, sometimes the formula forces you to take the square root of a negative number even when the final answer is a perfectly ordinary real number. The imaginary parts appear in the middle of the calculation and then cancel out at the end. The mathematician Rafael Bombelli, in 1572, was the first to say: fine, let's just treat these as if they follow the normal rules of algebra and see what happens. He followed the logic through and got the right real answer.

**Student:** So the imaginary numbers were like scaffolding — necessary to build the answer, even though they disappear from the final result.

**Professor:** A lovely metaphor. And it's part of why mathematicians eventually had to take them seriously. They weren't just some philosophical curiosity; ignoring them meant you couldn't finish the calculation.

**Student:** Is there a sense in which imaginary numbers are any less "real" than real numbers?

**Professor:** That question cuts to the heart of the philosophy of mathematics. What does it mean for a number to be "real"? Negative numbers were considered suspect for centuries — you can't have negative three apples. Irrational numbers disturbed the Pythagoreans so much that legend says they drowned the man who proved they existed. Zero was controversial. The history of mathematics is largely a history of expanding what we're willing to call a number.

**Student:** So "real" numbers aren't particularly more real than imaginary ones.

**Professor:** The name "real" is just as historical an accident as "imaginary." Both terms were coined before anyone understood the geometry. A more honest description might be: one-dimensional numbers versus two-dimensional numbers. Or numbers that live on a line versus numbers that live on a plane.

**Student:** And both are equally valid mathematical objects.

**Professor:** With the further irony that imaginary numbers turn out to be essential for describing physical reality — which is about as real as things get. The wavefunction in quantum mechanics is an inherently complex-valued object. Not real-valued with a little imaginary decoration on the side — genuinely, fundamentally complex.

**Student:** So Descartes called them imaginary because they seemed like a fiction, and it turns out they're indispensable for describing the actual universe.

**Professor:** Mathematics has a way of doing that. The things that seem most abstract, most disconnected from anything physical, have a habit of turning up exactly where they're needed. Non-Euclidean geometry was considered pure mathematical fantasy until Einstein used it to describe the shape of spacetime. Imaginary numbers were a notational inconvenience until they became the scaffolding of modern physics.

**Student:** I came in here annoyed at the name. I'm leaving convinced it's one of the best accidental jokes in the history of science.

**Professor:** The universe has a sense of humor. The "imaginary" numbers describe reality more completely than the "real" ones alone ever could.

---

*End of dialog*
