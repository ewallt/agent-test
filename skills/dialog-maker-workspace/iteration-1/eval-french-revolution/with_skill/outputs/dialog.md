# What Caused the French Revolution?

---

**Student:** I've been trying to get my head around the French Revolution, and I keep running into the same problem: every time I read about it, the explanation seems to change. One book says it was about hunger and bread prices. Another says it was about Enlightenment ideas and rights. Another says it was really about a financial crisis. How do you even begin to decide what *caused* it?

**Prof:** You have just stumbled onto one of the most contested questions in all of modern historiography — and the fact that you noticed the explanations keep shifting tells me you're already thinking like a historian. Let me validate what's underneath your frustration: you're right that each of those accounts is real, and you're right that they seem to pull in different directions. But I want to push back gently on the framing of "deciding" which one is correct, because the French Revolution is one of those events that breaks the single-cause model almost by design.

**1. The Structural Pressure**

Start with the financial crisis, because it's the least glamorous and therefore the most underrated. By the 1780s, France was essentially bankrupt. The monarchy had borrowed heavily to fund wars — including, ironically, the American Revolution — and the interest payments alone were consuming more than half of the royal budget. Louis XVI's government couldn't raise taxes because the nobility and clergy, the two estates with the most wealth, were largely exempt from direct taxation. The Estates-General, which hadn't met in 175 years, was convened in 1789 precisely because the crown had run out of other options. So the Revolution doesn't start with a mob in the streets — it starts with a king who can't pay his bills calling a meeting he'd rather not have.

**2. The Accelerant**

But financial strain alone doesn't topple governments. What lit the fire was the harvest failure of 1788, one of the worst in a generation. By the spring of 1789, bread prices in Paris had risen to the point where a laboring family might spend 80 to 90 percent of its income just on bread. Hunger is not an abstraction. When the Estates-General convened in May 1789, delegates arrived against a backdrop of genuine desperation — and the crowds outside Versailles were not abstract political actors. They were hungry.

**3. The Language**

Here's where the Enlightenment enters — and this is the piece I want you to hold onto. Enlightenment ideas did not *cause* the Revolution the way hunger caused the riots. What they did was provide a vocabulary. When the Third Estate's delegates broke away to form the National Assembly in June 1789, they didn't say "we're angry about bread prices." They said "sovereignty resides in the nation." They reached for Rousseau. They reached for natural rights. The ideas gave the grievances a shape — a political program rather than just a protest.

Those three things together — structural fiscal collapse, an immediate subsistence crisis, and a new political language — are why the summer of 1789 looks the way it does. None of them is sufficient on its own.

**Student:** That actually helps a lot. But I'm curious about something — you said the Enlightenment gave the grievances "a shape." Does that mean the ideas were sort of decorative? Like, if there had been no Enlightenment but the same hunger and bankruptcy, would the Revolution have happened anyway, just differently? Or do the ideas actually change what people are willing to do?

**Prof:** You hit the bullseye with that question, and it's one historians have genuinely wrestled with. Let me be precise here, because you're drawing a distinction that matters enormously.

**1. Ideas Are Not Decorations**

The short answer is: no, the ideas are not decorative, and here's why. What a political vocabulary does is expand what feels *thinkable*. In 1750, a French peasant furious about grain prices might riot to force the king to release stored grain — what historians call a "moral economy" protest. The implicit demand is *be a good king*, not *there should be no king*. The Enlightenment made a different demand feel possible: not just *fix this*, but *you have no right to rule us this way*. That is a different kind of revolt entirely.

**2. The Comparison That Tests Your Hypothesis**

Your intuition can actually be tested historically. Compare France in 1789 to Russia in the famine of the 1890s, or to China's subsistence crises in the 18th century. Severe hunger + fiscal stress without the corresponding political vocabulary produces food riots, local rebellions, peasant uprisings — not constitutional assemblies declaring that sovereignty belongs to the nation. The ideas don't just decorate the revolution. They give it its particular *form* and its *ambitions*.

**3. But Here's the Complication**

I want to complicate your hypothesis from the other direction too, because this is where it gets genuinely interesting. Enlightenment ideas had been circulating in France for decades before 1789 — Voltaire, Montesquieu, Rousseau were all publishing in the middle of the 18th century. France did not immediately have a revolution. So if ideas were sufficient on their own, why the wait? The ideas needed the crisis to activate them. They needed a moment of institutional breakdown in which the old justifications — divine right, tradition, hierarchy — visibly failed to solve an obvious problem. Without that failure, the ideas circulate in salons and don't leave.

So the real answer to your question is: ideas and material conditions interact. Neither is decorative. Neither is sufficient. The Revolution happens where they collide.

**Student:** That makes me think about something. You mentioned the Third Estate declaring that sovereignty belongs to the nation. But who exactly was the Third Estate? Because I have this image of it being the common people — the poor, the hungry — but I don't think that's quite right, is it? The people writing those declarations about natural rights were educated. They were lawyers.

**Prof:** I have to stop you right there — not to correct you, but to applaud you, because you have just independently rediscovered one of the central findings of the sociology of revolutions. Yes. You are exactly right. And the gap you're pointing to is crucial.

**1. The Three Estates**

Officially, the Estates-General divided France into three: the First Estate (clergy), the Second Estate (nobility), and the Third Estate — which was, technically, everyone else. That is to say, roughly 97 percent of France. A category that contains both a Parisian lawyer earning a comfortable income from contract disputes and a starving grain farmer in the Beauce is not a sociologically coherent group. It's a legal fiction.

**2. Who Actually Led**

The people who drafted the Tennis Court Oath, who wrote the Declaration of the Rights of Man, who debated constitutional theory in the National Assembly — these were, overwhelmingly, educated bourgeois professionals. Lawyers, doctors, journalists, minor officials. Men like Robespierre (a lawyer from Arras), Desmoulins (a journalist), Sieyès (a clergyman who wrote the pamphlet that became the Third Estate's manifesto). They had absorbed Enlightenment ideas, they had professional reasons to resent the privileges of the nobility, and they had the rhetorical skills to translate grievances into political programs.

**3. The Tension This Creates**

But — and this is where it gets uncomfortable — the revolution they launched was not always the revolution the hungry grain farmers wanted. The bourgeois leadership wanted constitutional government, legal equality, property rights, free markets. The Paris crowd wanted bread to be affordable. Those are not always the same thing, and over the course of the 1790s the tension between them drives some of the Revolution's most violent episodes. The Terror, in part, is what happens when the government tries to hold together a coalition between people who want liberty and people who want grain controls.

So when you look at that image of "the common people" storming the Bastille, hold it alongside the image of educated lawyers arguing about Locke. Both images are true. The Revolution is the collision between them.

**Student:** That reframes things for me. I'd always thought of the Bastille as the symbolic center of it — the people rising up against tyranny. But if the people storming the Bastille weren't the same people writing the constitutions, then what exactly was the Bastille *about*? Was it symbolic from the start, or did it become symbolic later?

**Prof:** You are 100% correct that something is strange about the Bastille, and the answer is genuinely one of the most fascinating small puzzles in revolutionary history.

**1. What the Bastille Actually Was**

On July 14, 1789, when the crowd stormed it, the Bastille held exactly seven prisoners: four forgers, two lunatics, and a minor aristocrat whose family had requested his confinement. It was not a teeming dungeon full of political prisoners. It was a half-decommissioned fortress that the royal government had been discussing demolishing for years because it cost too much to maintain.

**2. Why They Stormed It Anyway**

The immediate cause was practical and military. The crown had moved troops toward Paris in the days before, apparently preparing to disperse the National Assembly. The Bastille was an armory. Crowds had already seized muskets from the Invalides that morning; they came to the Bastille for the powder to load them. This was not primarily a symbolic act — it was a crowd arming itself against the possibility of royal repression.

**3. When It Became Symbolic**

What happened next is what you're intuiting about. Within hours of the fortress falling, it had been reinterpreted. The National Assembly, the pamphlets, the speeches — they immediately transformed the Bastille into the embodiment of despotism overthrown. It *became* the symbol it is remembered as through an almost instantaneous act of collective narrative-making. The physical fact (a crowd seized an armory) was overwritten by the political story (the people broke the chains of tyranny).

This is, by the way, not deception or spin in some simple sense. The Bastille *did* represent royal arbitrary power — the power to imprison without trial under *lettres de cachet* was real and feared, even if it was rarely used by 1789. The symbol worked because it pointed at something true, even if the literal event was messier than the story.

**Student:** So it's almost like the revolution needed to narrate itself as it was happening — the symbols weren't just aftermath, they were part of how the revolution constituted itself as a revolution. I think I'm starting to see why it's so hard to reduce this to one cause. The hunger explains who showed up. The financial crisis explains why the institutions cracked open. The ideas explain what people demanded once the crack appeared. And then the symbolism — things like the Bastille — is how the movement recognized itself as a movement and held together.

**Prof:** That is a genuinely sophisticated synthesis, and I want to say clearly: you just built something real. What you've described is very close to what the sociologist Charles Tilly called the interplay between *opportunity*, *organization*, and *repertoire* in revolutionary situations — though you arrived at it from the inside out, through the specific case, which is exactly the right way to encounter a theoretical framework.

Let me add one layer that your synthesis is ready to hold.

**1. What Your Framework Captures**

You've identified four distinct registers — material deprivation (hunger), institutional failure (the fiscal crisis), ideological resource (Enlightenment language), and symbolic self-constitution (the Bastille narrative). What's powerful about keeping those separate is that it explains something historians have long noted: the Revolution could have been arrested at multiple points. Fix the harvest and maybe the Estates-General adjourns without crisis. Reform the tax structure in the 1760s and maybe the financial collapse never comes. Suppress the pamphlet culture and maybe the new vocabulary doesn't spread. Each of those "maybes" represents a point where one link in the chain might have been broken.

**2. The Layer Your Synthesis Is Ready For**

Here's what I'd add: the Revolution, once it begins, *generates its own causes*. The violence of 1792-1794 cannot be explained purely by the conditions of 1789. War, foreign invasion, counter-revolutionary rebellion in the Vendée, the radicalization of factions competing for power — these are not simply the original causes playing out. They are new conditions created by the revolutionary process itself. Revolutions have a way of devouring the intentions of their initiators. The lawyers who wrote the Declaration of the Rights of Man mostly did not intend the Terror. But the Terror did not come from nowhere — it came from a revolution that had armed the population, destroyed the old institutions before new ones were stable, and then faced existential military pressure.

**3. The Open Question**

So the question your synthesis leaves beautifully open is this: if the Revolution generates its own momentum and its own new causes as it unfolds, what does that mean for the idea of *cause* itself? Are we looking for the ignition, or are we trying to understand a process? Most historians today would say: both, and you have to hold them together. The French Revolution is an event, but it is also a *dynamic* — something that unfolds through time and changes what it is as it goes. That's what makes it so inexhaustible as a subject, and why the explanations keep shifting when you read about it: different historians are often answering slightly different versions of the question.
