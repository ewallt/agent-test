# Structure Guide

The document has four parts. Follow this structure in order.

For a full worked example of everything below, see `references/example-matthew6.md`.

---

## Part 1 — Opening Framing

Two paragraphs:

**Paragraph 1 — The apparent reading.** State what a surface-level reader would think about this topic. What does it look like at first glance? What assumption does a casual observer make?

**Paragraph 2 — The real argument.** Pivot hard. "They are not." / "It is not." Announce what the document will actually show — the hidden logic, the unexpected structure, the deeper truth. End with a clear thesis statement.

The pivot is the engine of the opening. It establishes dramatic tension: here is what people think, and here is what is actually going on.

**Example:**
> "Matthew 6 looks, at first reading, like a miscellaneous collection of religious instructions loosely grouped under the heading of piety... A first-time reader could be forgiven for thinking these are simply adjacent teachings strung together by a compiler who ran out of other places to put them.
>
> They are not. Matthew 6 is a single, sustained argument — one of the most carefully constructed passages in the Gospels — and every section is a logical consequence of what precedes it."

---

## Part 2 — Numbered Movements

Typically 4–8 movements. Each covers one stage of the argument.

### Format for each movement

```
### Movement N — [Title]

**[Label]:** [Key text, quote, or central example]
**The question it answers:** [One sentence]

[Two to four paragraphs of analytical prose.]
```

The label depends on the topic — it might be "Passage:", "Example:", "Principle:", "Case:", etc. Choose whatever fits naturally.

The guiding question frames what this movement contributes to the overall argument. It should be a question a reader could actually ask.

**Prose standards for movements:**
- No bullet lists. Analytical paragraphs only.
- Each paragraph develops a single idea fully before moving on.
- Surface the non-obvious: etymology, historical context, counterintuitive findings, what the original audience would have understood that modern readers miss.
- Connect forward: each movement should make the next one feel inevitable.
- Movements earn their place by answering a question the previous movement raised.

**Example (Movement 4 from Matthew 6):**
> **Passage:** "Do not lay up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust destroy..."
> **The question it answers:** What are you actually accumulating?
>
> "The transition from the hidden disciplines to the treasure saying is more direct than it appears. The person who performs almsgiving, prayer, and fasting for human approval has been investing — not in God's economy but in social capital: reputation, admiration, status, the perception of holiness. These are earthly treasures. They are also perishable ones."

## Part 3 — Synthesis

Section title: **The Hidden Logic** (or adapt to fit the topic — e.g. "The Underlying Structure", "The Through-Line", "How It Connects").

This section names the meta-structure: the logical stages that run beneath the movements. Typically 3–5 named stages, each in **bold**, each with 1–2 sentences explaining its role in the argument.

Close with a paragraph showing the argument working in both directions — forward (as diagnosis) and backward (as prescription), or some equivalent bidirectional reading. This gives the content its closing force.

**Example:**
> "The first stage is **behavioral observation**... The second stage is **economic diagnosis**... The argument is remarkable because it runs in both directions. Understood forward, it is a diagnosis: anxious people should examine their treasure, their eye, their master. Understood backward, it is a prescription: people who want to be free of anxiety should begin not with anxiety management but with the question of where their security is actually located."

## Part 4 — Key Terms

A glossary of 6–12 terms. Choose terms that carry the argument's weight — words a reader needs to understand precisely to follow the content, not obvious words.

Each entry:
- **Term** — [definition]

Definitions should be 2–4 sentences minimum. Include etymology or original-language meaning where relevant. Explain how the term functions in *this specific argument*, not just what it means in general.

Avoid one-liners. The Key Terms section deepens understanding; it does not just label things.

**Example:**
> **Apechō** — Greek commercial term meaning "paid in full" or "received in full"; used three times in 6:2, 6:5, and 6:16 to describe the reward of those who perform religious acts for public approval; implies that the transaction is complete and no further payment is owed. The commercial register of the word is the point: the exchange is finished, the account closed, nothing further is coming.

---

## Overall Prose Standard

The document should read as a well-structured essay from start to finish, and also work as a navigable reference. These goals are compatible.

- Analytical prose throughout. Paragraphs, not outlines.
- Concrete over abstract: when making an analytical point, demonstrate it with a specific instance.
- Depth over breadth: one movement done fully is better than two movements done superficially.
- The document reveals something the reader did not know before reading it. If it only summarizes what is obvious, it has not done its job.
