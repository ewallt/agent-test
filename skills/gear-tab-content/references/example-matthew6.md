# Example Document: Where Your Heart Is — The Hidden Argument of Matthew 6

This is the canonical example of the gear-tab-content structure. Use it as the pattern reference when writing new content documents.

---

## The Apparent Problem

Matthew 6 looks, at first reading, like a miscellaneous collection of religious instructions loosely grouped under the heading of piety. Jesus teaches about almsgiving, then prayer, then fasting, then suddenly pivots to treasure, then to a cryptic saying about eyes and lamps, then to the impossibility of serving two masters, and then — with the word "therefore" — launches into a famous meditation on anxiety and the birds of the air. A first-time reader could be forgiven for thinking these are simply adjacent teachings strung together by a compiler who ran out of other places to put them.

They are not. Matthew 6 is a single, sustained argument — one of the most carefully constructed passages in the Gospels — and every section is a logical consequence of what precedes it. The argument moves from an observable behavior (performing religious acts for public approval) backward to its root cause (misplaced treasure), inward to its diagnostic sign (the condition of the eye), down to its governing allegiance (which master is actually being served), and finally to its emotional symptom (anxiety about material provision). Strip away the apparent diversity of topics and a single question emerges from every section: *where have you actually located your security?*

The word that makes this explicit is the one most readers glide past. Matthew 6:25 begins in Greek with *dia touto* — "for this reason," or "therefore." That word is a logical connector. Jesus is not changing the subject when he moves from the two-masters saying to the anxiety teaching. He is drawing a conclusion. What follows the "therefore" is the emotional consequence of everything that came before it.

## The Six Movements

### Movement 1 — The Hidden Disciplines: Almsgiving (6:1–4)

**Passage:** "When you give to the needy, do not announce it with trumpets, as the hypocrites do in the synagogues and on the streets, to be honored by others. Truly I tell you, they have received their reward in full."
**The question it answers:** Who is your intended audience when you act?

Jesus opens Matthew 6 with a principle that governs the entire chapter: be careful not to practice your righteousness *in front of others in order to be seen by them* (6:1). Every major section that follows is an application of this principle to a specific domain.

The example he reaches for first is almsgiving — charitable giving to the poor — which was one of the three central acts of Jewish piety in the first century, alongside prayer and fasting. The giving of alms was considered a duty so fundamental that the Hebrew word for it, *tzedakah*, is the same word as "righteousness." A person who gave was not doing something extra; they were fulfilling an obligation inherent to being in right relationship with God and community.

Jesus does not dispute this. His concern is not whether to give but what giving is *for*. He describes people who give in a manner that draws public attention — he uses the phrase "sound a trumpet before you," which may be literal (some scholars point to trumpet-shaped collection vessels in the Temple) or may be idiomatic for any ostentatious announcement. His verdict is precise: they have received their reward in full. The Greek word is *apechō* — a commercial term meaning a receipt has been issued, the account is settled, the transaction is complete. They performed for human applause. They received human applause. Nothing further is owed. The account between them and heaven remains empty.

The alternative — giving secretly, where only the Father sees — is not merely a technique for avoiding pride. It is a statement about audience. The person who gives secretly is performing for a different watcher. Their investment is in a different account. This is where the argument begins: the question of audience is the question of where you are storing your reward.

### Movement 2 — The Hidden Disciplines: Prayer (6:5–15)

**Passage:** "When you pray, do not be like the hypocrites, for they love to pray standing in the synagogues and on the street corners to be seen by others."
**The question it answers:** Who are you actually talking to?

The same logic extends from giving to praying. Jesus identifies a practice of prayer that is structurally public — not in the sense of communal worship, which he does not criticize, but in the sense of performing prayer as a display directed at human observers rather than at God. The hypocrites, he says, *love* to pray in conspicuous locations. The love is the problem. The act is not wrong; the audience orientation is wrong. Again: *apechō*. Their reward is paid in full by the admiration of bystanders.

The alternative he offers — "go into your room and shut the door and pray to your Father who is in secret" — deliberately removes the human audience. The point is not that private prayer is superior to public prayer in all circumstances, but that prayer that requires an audience to feel real has located its value in the wrong place. It is performing for human approval the same way that public almsgiving is performing for human approval. The two acts look very different on the surface; their interior dynamic is identical.

The Lord's Prayer, inserted here as a model, is notable in this context for its orientation. It opens not with petitions but with address and surrender — *your* name, *your* kingdom, *your* will. The movement of the prayer is from God's concerns to human needs, not the reverse. It is structurally the opposite of prayer-as-performance, which uses the form of addressing God while actually addressing a human audience.

### Movement 3 — The Hidden Disciplines: Fasting (6:16–18)

**Passage:** "When you fast, do not look somber as the hypocrites do, for they disfigure their faces to show others they are fasting."
**The question it answers:** Who are you performing your suffering for?

The third discipline completes the triptych and makes the pattern unmistakable. Fasting, even more than giving or prayer, is an act that can only be authenticated by the faster themselves — no one can verify from the outside whether a person has actually abstained from food. What makes public fasting remarkable, then, is that it requires active effort to make an invisible practice visible. The "disfigured faces" Jesus describes — unwashed, disheveled, arranged to communicate deprivation — represent deliberate performance. This is not a person who happens to look hungry; it is a person who has arranged their appearance to broadcast their hunger.

The reward structure is again identical: *apechō*. The performance has been seen. The transaction is closed.

By the end of 6:18, Jesus has made the same point three times across three domains. The repetition is not accidental. He is establishing a diagnostic principle that will carry the rest of the chapter: there is a way of doing religious acts that locates their value in the response of human observers, and there is a way that locates their value in the response of God. These two orientations are not just different styles; they represent investment in fundamentally different accounts. Which brings him to his next move.

---

### Movement 4 — Treasure (6:19–21)

**Passage:** "Do not lay up for yourselves treasures on earth, where moth and rust destroy and where thieves break in and steal, but lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven… For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also."
**The question it answers:** What are you actually accumulating?

The transition from the hidden disciplines to the treasure saying is more direct than it appears. The person who performs almsgiving, prayer, and fasting for human approval has been investing — not in God's economy but in social capital: reputation, admiration, status, the perception of holiness. These are earthly treasures. They are also perishable ones. Reputation can be destroyed overnight. Social standing can collapse. The admiration of a community can turn to contempt. Jesus's examples — moth, rust, thieves — make the same point across three domains: earthly treasure is insecure by nature, because the conditions that preserve it are outside your control.

The contrasting instruction is not asceticism — Jesus does not say to accumulate nothing. He says to accumulate in a different register, one where the conditions of preservation are secure. "Treasures in heaven" is not a reward deferred to the afterlife so much as a way of describing investment in what is genuinely lasting: relationship with God, acts of justice and mercy that participate in God's kingdom, the formation of character. Luther, commenting on this passage, observed: "What a man loves, that is his God. For he carries it in his heart, he goes about with it night and day, he sleeps and wakes with it."

The final line of this section — "where your treasure is, there your heart will be also" — is often read as a warning: be careful what you love. But it is also a diagnostic: what you actually love can be detected by observing where you have invested. Behavior reveals allegiance. The person who has spent the last decade accumulating social prestige through conspicuous religious performance has revealed where their heart is, regardless of what they say about their spiritual priorities.

### Movement 5 — The Eye as Lamp (6:22–23)

**Passage:** "The eye is the lamp of the body. If your eye is good, your whole body will be full of light. But if your eye is bad, your whole body will be full of darkness."
**The question it answers:** What does your orientation toward money reveal about your inner life?

This is the passage that most disoriented readers are likely to stumble over, because English translations have largely obscured the Hebrew idiom on which it depends. "Good eye" and "bad eye" — in Greek *ophthalmos haplous* and *ophthalmos poneros* — are calques of the Hebrew phrases *ayin tovah* (good eye) and *ayin ra'ah* (bad eye), idioms that appear throughout the Hebrew Bible and were in everyday use in first-century Jewish speech. They have nothing to do with optics. They describe a person's attitude toward their money and their neighbor.

To have a *good eye* — *ayin tovah* — was to be generous, to look on the needs of others and respond. Proverbs 22:9: "A generous man (*tov ayin*) will be blessed, because he gives some of his food to the poor." To have a *bad eye* — *ayin ra'ah* — was to be stingy, to see the needs of others and contract around your resources. Deuteronomy 15:9 warns against letting a *ra'ah ayin* cause you to withhold help from a needy brother. Proverbs 23:6 warns against eating the bread of a *ra'ah ayin* person — they say "eat and drink" while their heart is not with you. Jesus uses the same idiom in Matthew 20:15, where the landowner says to the complaining workers: "Is your eye evil (*poneros*) because I am good?" — meaning, are you being stingy with my generosity?

When the idiom is restored, the saying slots directly into the flow of Matthew 6's argument. Jesus has just said that where your treasure is, your heart will be also. Now he adds: the condition of your eye — whether you see others generously or stingily — is the interior indicator of what your treasure really is. A person full of light is a person whose orientation toward money and neighbor is open and generous. A person full of darkness has turned inward, contracting around their resources, blind to the needs around them. The saying is not a digression into mysticism; it is a diagnostic of the heart's actual allegiance, expressed through the most everyday test available: how you relate to money in the presence of need.

### Movement 6 — Two Masters (6:24)

**Passage:** "No one can serve two masters. Either you will hate the one and love the other, or you will be devoted to the one and despise the other. You cannot serve both God and mammon."
**The question it answers:** Which allegiance is actually governing your life?

This is the shortest section in the chapter and the most explicit. Everything the previous sections have been circling — the question of audience, the question of investment, the question of where the eye is directed — is here stated in the starkest possible terms. You cannot divide your governing allegiance. You cannot have two ultimate loyalties. Whoever or whatever you actually serve is your master, and the other candidate is by definition a secondary concern, however sincerely you protest otherwise.

"Mammon" is an Aramaic word that can refer to wealth or property but more broadly to anything that functions as the object of human security and trust. It is personified here — not as an abstract force but as a rival claimant to the loyalty that belongs to God. The choice Jesus presents is not between wealth and poverty, but between two fundamentally different answers to the question: *where is my security located?* A person whose security is located in God can possess material things without being enslaved to them. A person whose security is located in material things is a slave to mammon regardless of how devoutly they pray.

The two-masters saying makes explicit what the treasure, eye, and disciplines passages have been building toward: this is a question of lordship, not merely of behavior. The person who performs religious acts for human approval has made human approval their functional lord. The person who stores earthly treasure has made security-through-accumulation their functional lord. The diagnosis runs deeper than behavior — it runs to the question of what is actually doing the governing.

---

### Movement 7 — Therefore: Anxiety (6:25–34)

**Passage:** "Therefore I tell you, do not be anxious about your life, what you will eat or what you will drink, or about your body, what you will put on."
**The question it answers:** What does your anxiety reveal, and how is it cured?

The chapter's final movement begins with *dia touto* — "for this reason," "therefore" — a Greek inferential conjunction that the fourth-century bishop John Chrysostom identified, and many modern scholars have confirmed, as the explicit logical link between the two-masters saying and the anxiety teaching. Jesus is not changing the subject. He is drawing a conclusion. *Because* you cannot serve two masters, *therefore* do not be anxious about material provision. The reasoning is as follows: if God is your master, God is your provider, and anxiety about provision is both unnecessary and a sign that your allegiance has drifted.

The Greek word translated "anxious" or "worried" is *merimnaō*, derived from a root meaning to be divided or pulled in two directions simultaneously. The word itself contains the diagnosis. Anxiety in Jesus's analysis is not primarily a psychological condition but a theological one — it is the emotional experience of trying to serve two masters. A person whose security is genuinely located in God does not experience the grinding, future-oriented dread of mammon-dependence, because their security is not vulnerable to the conditions that make material things precarious.

Jesus's arguments from nature — the birds who do not sow or reap yet are fed, the lilies that do not toil or spin yet surpass Solomon's glory — are not naive dismissals of prudent planning. He is making a theological argument about the character of the Father and the logic of creation. God provides for creatures with no capacity for self-provision. The disciples are not only creatures but children. How much more will their Father provide for them? The argument is from the lesser to the greater — a standard rabbinic form called *kal va-homer* — and its point is to reframe the disciple's fundamental orientation from scarcity to abundance.

The passage closes at 6:33 with an instruction that gathers the chapter's entire argument into a single imperative: "Seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and all these things will be added to you." The chapter that began with a warning about performing righteousness for the wrong audience ends with a command to seek the right thing with the right priority. Performance for human approval has been replaced by pursuit of the kingdom. Earthly treasure has been replaced by heavenly treasure. The bad eye has been replaced by the good eye. The divided master has been replaced by the single Lord. And anxiety — the emotional symptom of a divided heart — has been replaced by trust.

## The Hidden Logic

The argument of Matthew 6 moves through four stages that are not announced but are recoverable.

The first stage is **behavioral observation**: the hidden disciplines section (6:1–18) identifies a pattern of religious behavior oriented toward human approval rather than divine relationship. The diagnosis is behavioral — you can see it in how people give, pray, and fast — but the problem runs deeper than behavior.

The second stage is **economic diagnosis**: the treasure section (6:19–21) goes beneath behavior to investment. What you are accumulating reveals what you actually value. The person performing righteousness for public approval is accumulating social capital — an earthly treasure, perishable, insecure. The behavior has exposed the investment strategy.

The third stage is **internal diagnosis**: the eye passage (6:22–23) and the two-masters saying (6:24) go beneath investment to orientation and allegiance. The condition of the eye — whether you see others with generosity or with contraction — is the most intimate available indicator of what your heart is actually attached to. And beneath even that: which master is actually doing the governing? Behavior can be modified; orientation is harder to fake.

The fourth stage is **emotional consequence**: the anxiety section (6:25–34) names the emotional experience of misplaced allegiance. Anxiety is what divided loyalty feels like from the inside. It is the lived experience of trying to secure your life through resources that are inherently insecure. The cure Jesus offers is not a psychological technique but a theological reorientation: seek first the kingdom, and the anxiety will follow the allegiance into its proper place.

What makes this argument remarkable is that it runs in both directions. Understood forward, it is a diagnosis: anxious people should examine their treasure, their eye, their master. Understood backward, it is a prescription: people who want to be free of anxiety should begin not with anxiety management but with the question of where their security is actually located. The emotional symptom is downstream from the theological allegiance. Treat the allegiance; the symptom changes.

## Key Terms

- **Apechō** — Greek commercial term meaning "paid in full" or "received in full"; used three times in 6:2, 6:5, and 6:16 to describe the reward of those who perform religious acts for public approval; implies that the transaction is complete and no further payment is owed
- **Tzedakah** — Hebrew word meaning both "almsgiving" and "righteousness"; in first-century Judaism, charitable giving was not a supererogatory act but a component of being in right relationship with God and community
- **Mammon** — Aramaic term for wealth, property, or more broadly any object of human trust and security; personified by Jesus as a rival claimant to divine lordship; the root meaning is approximately "that in which one trusts"
- **Ayin tovah** — Hebrew: "good eye"; an idiom meaning generosity, attentiveness to the needs of others, and openness with material resources; used throughout the Hebrew Bible and in everyday first-century speech
- **Ayin ra'ah** — Hebrew: "bad eye"; the counterpart idiom to *ayin tovah*; means stinginess, self-centeredness, and contraction around one's resources in the presence of others' need
- **Merimnaō** — Greek: "to be anxious" or "to worry"; the root suggests division or being pulled in two directions simultaneously; used six times in Matthew 6:25–34; in Jesus's analysis names the emotional experience of divided allegiance rather than a merely psychological state
- **Dia touto** — Greek: "for this reason" or "therefore"; the inferential conjunction at the opening of 6:25 that explicitly connects the anxiety teaching to everything that preceded it; the word that reveals the passage as a single argument rather than a collection of sayings
- **Kal va-homer** — Hebrew: "light and heavy"; a standard rabbinic form of argument from the lesser to the greater; Jesus uses it in the birds and lilies passages — if God provides for creatures of lesser value, how much more will he provide for children of greater value
- **Hypokritēs** — Greek: "hypocrite"; originally referred to a stage actor — one who plays a role in a performance; Jesus uses it in 6:2, 6:5, and 6:16 to describe people whose religious practice is a performance directed at a human audience rather than a genuine orientation toward God
- **Kingdom of God / Kingdom of Heaven** — the governing reality that Jesus presents as the alternative object of seeking; in 6:33 its pursuit is given explicit priority over material provision; the term encompasses both present ethical reality and eschatological hope
- **Treasures in heaven** — not primarily a reference to posthumous reward but a description of investment in what genuinely lasts: relationship with God, acts of justice and mercy, the formation of character toward the kingdom; contrasted with earthly treasures subject to moth, rust, and theft
- **Merimnaō** — Greek: "to be anxious" or "to worry"; the root suggests division or being pulled in two directions simultaneously; used six times in Matthew 6:25–34; in Jesus's analysis names the emotional experience of divided allegiance rather than a merely psychological state
